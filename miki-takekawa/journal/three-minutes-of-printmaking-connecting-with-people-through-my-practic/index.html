Three Minutes of Printmaking: Connecting with People Through My Practice

By Miki Takekawa

Three Minutes of Printmaking: My Weekend at the London Illustration Fair

21–22 November 2026 | Copeland Park, Peckham

Over the weekend, I exhibited my work at the London Illustration Fair, where I also offered a free botanical printmaking activity alongside my original prints.

Established in 2013, the London Illustration Fair is an annual, artist-led event bringing together illustrators, printmakers, graphic designers and independent creatives. It provides opportunities for artists to sell their work directly to the public, exchange ideas and introduce people to their creative processes.

This year's fair brought together a programme of over 100 illustrators at Unit 08 in Copeland Park, Peckham, South East London.

Copeland Park and the neighbouring Bussey Building are home to a creative community of independent businesses, artists' studios, galleries and workshops. It's interesting to see how these former industrial spaces have developed into places where people can come together through creativity.

With our Community Arts workshops also taking place within Southwark, I think there is a lot we can learn from the different ways creative communities operate within the borough.

For me, the weekend was an opportunity to explore the relationship between exhibiting my work and encouraging people to participate in making art themselves.

Bringing My Artistic Practice into the Fair

Alongside displaying and selling my original monoprints, I wanted to give visitors an opportunity to experience a small part of my creative process.

Botanical printmaking is quite a tactile practice. I work with the natural shapes, textures and structures of plants, and I'm often interested in the unexpected qualities that emerge when transferring their impressions onto paper.

I wanted to introduce this experience to people without making it too complicated or requiring them to have any previous printmaking experience.

The organisers of the London Illustration Fair also encourage artists to incorporate interactive elements into their exhibitions, which I think makes the event quite different from a conventional art market.

Rather than only looking at finished artworks, visitors can discover something about how they were made and develop a more personal understanding of the artist's practice.

Creating a Three-Minute Printmaking Experience

There were so many different things happening at the fair that I wanted to make the activity as approachable as possible. I designed it so that someone could make their own botanical print in approximately three minutes.

It was almost more of a printmaking experience than a conventional workshop.

I didn't want people to feel that they had to commit a large amount of their time. There were many other artists and activities for them to discover, so I kept the process simple.

I would ask participants to choose two colours, guide them through arranging the botanical materials and help them transfer their impressions onto paper.

I often told people that it would only take three minutes. I think communicating this helped remove some of the hesitation around participating.

Something I noticed was that once somebody started making a print, other people would naturally stop to see what was happening. Sometimes they would watch for a while before asking if they could try it themselves.

It created quite a nice cycle of engagement. The workshop became something that people could discover through watching other people participate.

There were even moments when I was helping someone make a print while another person was waiting to purchase one of my artworks. The different activities seemed to generate momentum for one another.

Understanding Different Motivations

I had quite a few children participate throughout the weekend, particularly on Sunday.

They would often notice the activity first, watch what I was doing, and then their parents would encourage them to have a go.

Some parents helped their children by talking them through the different stages, while I assisted with the physical process of pressing and transferring the print.

What I found particularly interesting was the difference between how children and adults approached the activity.

Most of the children didn't seem to have any particular expectations about what their prints should look like. They were curious about the process and were generally very happy when they saw the outcome.

Some adults approached it differently. One participant had quite high expectations of what she wanted to produce and made several prints before finding an outcome she was happy with. The unpredictable nature of monoprinting seemed to cause her some frustration initially.

It made me think about how our expectations can sometimes interfere with our enjoyment of making things.

One of the things I personally enjoy about monoprinting is that I cannot completely control the outcome. Even when I use the same materials and colours, each print has its own qualities.

I would like to encourage participants to become more comfortable with that uncertainty, rather than feeling that something has gone wrong when the result is different from what they imagined.

Not Making Assumptions About People

Another important thing I learned was not to assume that someone isn't interested simply because they haven't approached me.

Although I had a sign explaining that the workshop was free, most people didn't directly ask me whether they could participate. I usually had to initiate the conversation myself.

Some people might be shy, while others might not immediately understand what the activity involves. At a busy fair, there is also a lot of information to take in.

I think there is a difference between making an activity available and actively making people feel welcome to participate in it.

This is something I can relate to our Community Arts workshops at Draper Hall. Sometimes people need an invitation before they feel comfortable sitting down and making something, even when the opportunity is already there.

At the same time, I think it's important to respect people's decisions. Not everybody who stops needs to participate. Watching someone else make a print can also be an interesting experience.

As a facilitator, I would like to become better at recognising these different forms of engagement without making assumptions about what people want.

Finding Materials on My Way to the Fair

I didn't purchase any new botanical materials for the workshop. Some of the leaves I used were collected on my way to the fair, and I also brought dried flowers that I already had.

Dried flowers are particularly useful because they can be prepared in advance. I can select varieties that I know will produce interesting impressions and are relatively easy for someone without printmaking experience to work with.

Using freshly collected leaves introduces something slightly different. It connects the activity to the season and to the environment around us.

I like that the materials I collected on my journey to Peckham eventually became part of artworks made by other people.

This is closely related to my own artistic practice. I'm interested in the small things that we tend to overlook in everyday life and how we can discover something beautiful in them by looking a little more closely.

A leaf that somebody might walk past without noticing can become the subject of an artwork.

I also hope that using these materials communicates to people that printmaking doesn't necessarily require expensive or difficult-to-find resources. They can begin exploring with things that are already available around them.

There is something quite nice about being able to continue a creative activity outside of the environment in which you first experienced it.

Making Small Printed Cards

I had quite a lot of leftover paper from my practice, which turned out to be ideal for making miniature printed cards.

The format was also practical for the fair. Participants could hold the unprinted section of the card while the ink was drying, making it easier for them to carry their artwork away without smudging it.

I like the idea of making something small that someone can immediately take home. It gives them a tangible reminder of the experience and potentially something they can share with another person.

A card already has quite a familiar purpose. It can be given to somebody, written inside or kept as a small personal artwork.

It has also made me interested in exploring other small formats, such as miniature zines and folded booklets, in future workshops.

At Community Arts, we could potentially develop this into a collaborative activity where participants contribute individual prints to a larger collection.

I think this would also be an interesting way of documenting workshops while allowing each participant to retain their own creative identity within a collective artwork.

Making the Exhibition Interactive

I had a limited amount of table space, so I needed to consider how to accommodate both my artworks and the workshop materials.

I used the wall to display some of my prints and placed others in baskets where people could look through them individually.

The baskets worked particularly well. It reminded me of looking through records in a vinyl shop, where part of the enjoyment comes from discovering something that you personally connect with.

Because each of my prints is different, people could spend time comparing them and finding their own favourite.

The artworks on the wall helped attract attention, but the baskets and the printmaking activity gave people a reason to stay.

At an event with so many illustrators exhibiting, getting somebody to stop at your table is quite an important first step. Once they stop, there is more opportunity to have a conversation and introduce them to your work.

This made me think about exhibiting artwork as something that can involve touch, movement, discovery and conversation, rather than only looking at something from a distance.

The tactile element of browsing through the prints complemented the tactile nature of the workshop itself.

Communicating More Through the Exhibition Space

One limitation of my exhibition was the amount of information I could communicate within the available space.

I wanted visitors to enjoy both the artworks and the workshop, but this meant that there wasn't much room to introduce the wider context of my practice.

If I had more space, I think I would prioritise introducing clearer information rather than necessarily making the workshop area larger.

I would like to explain why I work with botanical materials, what interests me about monoprinting and how I use these techniques when facilitating workshops.

I would also like to introduce people to my involvement with Community Arts and the different opportunities for creative participation we are developing.

During busy periods, it was difficult to have these more detailed conversations while also facilitating the activity and selling my work.

Having information available for people to read independently could allow those who are particularly interested to discover more about my practice without needing to rely entirely on a conversation with me.

Considering the Environmental Impact of My Practice

One thing I am still trying to find a solution to is the use of plastic sleeves to protect my prints.

I've previously tried displaying prints without protective packaging because I'm conscious of the environmental impact of plastic. However, I found that this made it more difficult to sell the artworks.

There is a balance between making environmentally responsible decisions and maintaining the presentation and protection that people expect when purchasing original artwork.

Especially when people are encouraged to handle and browse through prints, the packaging serves quite an important practical purpose.

My use of collected botanical materials and leftover paper allows me to reduce waste during the making process, but I would also like to consider what happens after the artwork has been made.

I would like to investigate recyclable or alternative packaging materials that could meet both of these needs without compromising the experience of browsing through the prints.

Bringing Artistic Practice and Facilitation Together

One of the most rewarding aspects of the weekend was being able to move between explaining my artworks and facilitating the activity.

The workshop was essentially an introduction to a technique that I use within my own practice. This meant that when someone was interested in one of my finished prints, I could also explain how it was made and invite them to experience part of that process themselves.

As an artist, I think it's important to have opportunities to share our own creative interests alongside teaching.

Facilitation can sometimes be quite demanding because there are so many elements that are outside of our control. Being able to exhibit my own work alongside the workshop made the experience personally rewarding as well.

I wasn't only introducing people to an activity. I was also communicating something about myself as an artist, what interests me and how I approach making work.

I think this could be an interesting format for future Community Arts events, particularly for student facilitators who are developing their own artistic practices.

What Could This Mean for Community Arts?

The experience has given me some ideas about how we could develop future events at Draper Hall and potentially other spaces within Southwark.

Many student artists are developing their own creative identities while also looking for opportunities to gain experience in teaching and facilitation.

I think it would be quite motivating for them to have opportunities to exhibit their work alongside delivering workshops that are informed by their individual practices.

Rather than separating exhibiting from teaching, we could create environments where the two support each other.

Visitors would have the opportunity to discover an artist's work, speak to them about their interests and experience some of their creative process for themselves.

The artists would also have an opportunity to develop their professional experience, communicate their ideas and build relationships with people outside of their immediate artistic circles.

What I find particularly interesting about the London Illustration Fair is how it brings together professional artistic practice and public participation within the same environment.

There are elements of this approach that I would like us to explore on a smaller, more community-focused scale.

For me, the weekend was a reminder that sharing art doesn't always need to involve a complicated activity or a long explanation.

Sometimes three minutes, a few leaves and a conversation are enough to create a meaningful connection.
