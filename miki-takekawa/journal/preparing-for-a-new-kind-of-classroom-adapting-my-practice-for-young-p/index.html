Preparing for a New Kind of Classroom: Adapting My Practice for Young People

By Miki Takekawa

Next Wednesday, I will be visiting Long Road Sixth Form College in Cambridge to deliver two creative workshops for teenagers as part of an outreach opportunity through Camberwell College of Arts.

There will be a morning and an afternoon session, with approximately 20 to 25 students in each group. This will be the largest group of participants I have worked with so far, as my previous workshops have generally involved no more than 15 people at a time.

I'm looking forward to the opportunity, but it has also made me think carefully about how I can adapt my existing approach to facilitation for a different audience, a larger group and an unfamiliar environment.

Working with Unfamiliar Materials

My original proposal involved using rollers and printing inks, which are materials I'm already familiar with through my own practice.

However, after discussing the practical arrangements with staff at Camberwell, we decided that this approach might be too messy and difficult to manage with the number of students participating.

Instead, we're exploring the use of Riso masters, which can allow ink to be transferred onto paper through pressure and mark-making.

I haven't had the opportunity to experiment with the materials yet, so part of my preparation will involve understanding their properties and discovering what kinds of outcomes are possible.

Although this means restructuring some of my original workshop plans, I think it could also be an interesting opportunity to explore a technique outside of my usual practice.

I'm particularly interested in whether I can introduce cut-outs, botanical forms and other intuitive approaches to composition, rather than simply asking students to produce a conventional drawing.

I want the materials to encourage experimentation and allow students to discover something through the process itself.

Thinking About the Teenage Audience

Most of the presentation materials I have developed for my previous workshops were intended for adults. Preparing for this session has made me reconsider how I introduce my work and communicate creative ideas to younger people.

I imagine that some of the students might initially feel quite shy or uncertain about what they're being asked to do, particularly if they aren't familiar with my practice or the technique.

I would like to begin with a simple icebreaker, similar to approaches I have used in previous workshops, including my experience at Haifa Studio.

Rather than immediately expecting everyone to produce an artwork, I think it would be helpful to give them a small opportunity to become familiar with the materials and feel more comfortable within the group.

I also want to share some of my experience as a student at Camberwell and introduce examples of my own work. I hope this will give the students a better understanding of how a relatively simple creative process can develop into a more personal artistic practice.

Providing Guidance Without Restricting Creativity

Something I have been thinking about since the art fair is the importance of limiting the number of decisions participants need to make at the beginning of an activity.

During the fair, I intentionally kept the process quite simple. I would ask someone to choose two colours, introduce the next step and gradually guide them through making their print.

Giving people too many options at once can sometimes make an activity more difficult to approach, especially when they are unfamiliar with the materials.

However, I don't want the students to feel that they are simply following instructions to reproduce something I have already made.

I think there is a balance between providing enough structure for someone to feel confident and giving them enough freedom to develop their own ideas.

This is something we regularly explore during our Community Arts workshops at Draper Hall. We might demonstrate a technique or introduce a particular material, but the outcomes are often quite different from one another because everyone approaches the activity in their own way.

I would like to bring that same openness into the college workshop.

Encouraging Experimentation Rather Than Perfection

My experience at the art fair also made me more aware of the expectations that people bring into creative activities.

Some participants are immediately comfortable experimenting, while others become quite focused on whether the final outcome will meet their expectations.

I wonder how this might translate into an educational environment, where students are perhaps more accustomed to having their work assessed or compared with particular examples.

I would like the workshop to provide a different kind of experience, where an unexpected outcome can become a starting point for another idea rather than something that needs to be corrected.

Within my own practice, I often discover interesting qualities in an artwork that I hadn't originally intended to create. The physical properties of the materials contribute to the outcome, and I enjoy responding to what happens during the process.

I hope to communicate some of this through the workshop.

Rather than presenting a finished example as something that everyone should aim to replicate, I would like to introduce several possibilities and encourage the students to discover their own.

Preparing for a Larger Group

Working with approximately 25 students at once will be quite different from the smaller workshops I have facilitated previously.

In a smaller group, I can usually spend time with each participant, respond to individual questions and adapt the activity as it develops.

With a larger group, I will need to think more carefully about the organisation of the materials, the clarity of my demonstrations and how independently the students can work after receiving instructions.

There will be approximately an hour and a half available for the practical activity, alongside introductions and a presentation about my experience at Camberwell.

I don't usually take a particularly systematic approach to planning my workshops, but I think this experience will benefit from having a clearer structure in advance.

Having a framework for the session could allow me to concentrate more on engaging with the students rather than making too many practical decisions while facilitating.

At the same time, I want to leave enough flexibility to respond to the interests and abilities of each group.

Although I will be delivering the same workshop twice, I'm interested to see how the two sessions might develop differently depending on the students and their responses to the materials.

Botanical Materials and Accessibility

I'm also considering how to source enough botanical materials for the number of students participating.

Through Community Arts, we have discussed the possibility of approaching Walworth Garden to explore whether they might be able to support the workshop with suitable plant materials.

I still need to discuss the practical arrangements and available budget with the staff at Camberwell.

One of the things I value about working with botanical materials is that they are accessible and familiar, even to someone who has never participated in printmaking before.

Leaves and flowers have their own structures, textures and patterns. They provide an immediate starting point for experimentation without requiring someone to have developed technical drawing skills.

I also think they offer an opportunity to encourage students to become more observant of their surroundings.

I'm interested in how creative activities can change our relationship with ordinary objects. Something that might initially seem insignificant can become interesting when we spend time examining its shape, texture or structure.

Exploring the Role of the Body in Making

Another area that I would like to explore further within my workshops is the relationship between creativity and physical experience.

Although I don't necessarily introduce imagery of the human body into my existing workshops, the act of making is already quite tactile.

Participants use their hands to arrange materials, apply pressure and transfer impressions. Small differences in these physical actions can change the appearance of an artwork.

I'm interested in how we could develop this further in future workshops, perhaps by introducing movement, quick observational drawing or working with dancers and life drawing models.

These could become interesting warm-up activities, encouraging participants to respond intuitively to movement and form before developing their ideas through other materials.

I think there is something valuable about engaging with art through the body, rather than approaching creativity entirely as a visual or intellectual activity.

Developing My Practice as a Facilitator

Preparing for this workshop has made me reflect on how much my experience as a facilitator continues to inform my development as an artist.

Every group introduces different challenges. The participants, materials, environment and available time all influence how an activity develops.

My experiences at Draper Hall, the art fair and previous workshops have each taught me something slightly different about communicating creative ideas.

I'm beginning to understand that being able to make an artwork and being able to introduce somebody else to that process are two different skills.

Facilitation requires me to reconsider techniques that have become familiar to me and understand them from the perspective of someone encountering them for the first time.

It also encourages me to question which elements of my practice are essential and which can be adapted or simplified without losing what makes the process interesting.

I'm looking forward to seeing how the students respond next Wednesday and what I can learn from working with them.

I hope that the experience will help me develop more adaptable approaches to facilitation, which I can bring back into my work with Community Arts and future creative workshops.
