Growing our own colour

We visited the Horniman Dye Garden to learn more about plant-based dyes and consider how we could develop their use in our workshops. I have a growing interest in history, and making our own colours has brought that interest into the practical work we’re doing.

I’ve always been fascinated by how a colour could carry social meaning because of the difficulty of producing it. (Tyrian purple, for example, extracted from marine molluscs through a demanding process, became associated with royalty and status). Something we now choose almost without thinking once communicated access to extraordinary resources and labour.

The availability of colour has changed through trade, the movement of knowledge and developments in chemistry. The industrial production of synthetic dyes, including Perkin’s mauve in the nineteenth century, helped transform how colours could be manufactured and supplied. That history gives another dimension to experimenting with a cup of plant extract: the relationship between a material, the knowledge needed to use it and the people who can access it.

Visiting the garden brought these interests together. I wanted to understand the plants behind the colours and think about how that knowledge could become part of our sessions. Preparing a dye could give participants something to investigate together, alongside the drawing, printing or lettering that follows.

It also gave me the idea of using one of our gardens to grow dye plants ourselves. There would be a lot to learn about cultivation, harvesting and preparation, but I’m interested in developing a renewable source of materials for the workshops. Following a colour from a growing plant to a mark on paper could become a shared project in its own right.
