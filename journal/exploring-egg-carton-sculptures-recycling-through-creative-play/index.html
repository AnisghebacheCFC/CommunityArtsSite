Exploring Egg Carton Sculptures: Recycling Through Creative Play

Written by Anna Verdes


During one of our Community Arts workshops, Alma, a regular participant, introduced an interesting question about how we might repurpose discarded egg cartons.

Alma has participated in several of our workshops, and I've always appreciated her curiosity and willingness to experiment. She's particularly receptive to exploring unfamiliar materials, colours and textures, and brings a lovely sense of enthusiasm and collaboration to our sessions.

Her question concerned whether egg cartons could be transformed into something creative and accessible for children. She was interested in activities that might be suitable for the children attending her local church, where opportunities for creative engagement are organised over the weekends.

I thought this was a wonderful starting point for an experiment.

Egg cartons are inexpensive, widely available and frequently discarded. Their fibrous structure also suggested that they might be manipulated into something resembling a sculptural material.

I decided to explore whether they could be transformed into small, playful objects using relatively simple techniques.

Experimenting with the Material

I began by breaking down the egg carton material and introducing water to soften its structure.

As the cardboard absorbed moisture, it became increasingly malleable, allowing me to manipulate it into different shapes.

I then introduced PVA glue as a binding agent, experimenting with the proportions until I found a consistency that was sufficiently soft to sculpt while remaining adhesive enough to hold separate pieces together.

One of the more interesting qualities of the mixture was its balance between malleability and stickiness.

Initially, the material was rather wet, but as I adjusted the ratio between the softened cardboard and PVA, it became easier to manage.

Unlike conventional clay, which can sometimes require considerable pressure to manipulate, the softened cardboard offered relatively little resistance.

This seemed particularly promising from an accessibility perspective.

For participants who experience discomfort or fatigue when working with firmer sculptural materials, a softer alternative could make the activity more approachable.

I was also interested in how easily separate elements could be attached.

When working with clay, joining pieces often requires preparing the surfaces and introducing moisture to establish a secure connection. With this mixture, the adhesive properties of the PVA allowed pieces to be brought together relatively quickly.

This provided a more immediate sense of progress, which I thought could be particularly appealing when working with children.

Creating the Sculptures

To explore the possibilities of the material, I created several small figures, including a pumpkin-like character, a turtle and a face.

I wanted to experiment with forms that were relatively uncomplicated but expressive enough to demonstrate how the material might be used creatively.

The resulting sculptures had a distinctive handmade appearance.

Their slightly irregular surfaces and soft forms gave them a playful quality that I found rather charming.

Rather than attempting to produce perfectly smooth or symmetrical objects, I enjoyed allowing the characteristics of the material to influence their appearance.

This is something I particularly appreciate about working with recycled materials.

Their original properties remain evident in the finished work, creating a relationship between the material's previous purpose and its new identity.

An ordinary egg carton can become something entirely different through a relatively simple process of manipulation and imagination.

Encountering a Practical Challenge

Although I was pleased with the sculptures themselves, I encountered an important practical limitation.

I had completed the experiment the day before our next Community Arts session, hoping to bring the finished objects along to show Alma.

Unfortunately, the mixture retained considerably more moisture than I'd anticipated.

By the following day, the sculptures hadn't dried sufficiently for me to transport and present them as finished examples.

I shared photographs of the results with Anis, but the experience highlighted something that would need to be addressed before introducing the activity into a workshop.

Although the material was easy to manipulate, its drying time presented a challenge.

For a workshop involving children, particularly one conducted within a limited period, it would be useful for participants to experience a relatively immediate sense of accomplishment.

The sculpting process itself offered this through the ease with which forms could be created and joined. However, the time required for the finished objects to dry could complicate how the activity is structured.

I'd therefore like to investigate whether adjusting the amount of water and PVA, removing excess moisture or producing smaller, thinner sculptures might improve the drying process.

Alternatively, the activity could be organised across multiple sessions, allowing the sculptures to dry before participants return to decorate them.

These possibilities remain to be tested.

Considering Accessibility and Future Workshops

What interested me most about this experiment was its potential as an accessible creative activity.

The materials are inexpensive and relatively easy to obtain, making the process particularly appealing for community settings where budgets and resources may be limited.

The softness of the mixture could also accommodate participants with different levels of confidence and physical dexterity.

I think there's considerable value in activities that allow people to experiment without requiring extensive technical knowledge or specialised equipment.

For children, the ability to manipulate a material and quickly assemble recognisable forms could provide an enjoyable introduction to three-dimensional art.

There is also an educational dimension to the process.

By transforming discarded packaging into imaginative objects, participants can experience recycling as an act of creative transformation rather than simply a matter of waste disposal.

I particularly like the possibility of encouraging children to look at everyday objects differently, recognising that materials which appear to have exhausted their original purpose might still offer creative opportunities.

Reflections

This experiment emerged from a simple question raised by Alma, but it encouraged me to consider several aspects of workshop development.

I was reminded of the importance of listening to participants and allowing their suggestions to influence the activities we explore.

Although we often arrive at workshops with prepared ideas, some of the most interesting possibilities emerge through conversation and collaboration.

Alma's curiosity provided the initial inspiration, and my experiment became an opportunity to investigate how that idea might be translated into a practical activity.

I'm pleased with the playful qualities of the sculptures and particularly interested in the accessibility offered by the material's softness and adhesive properties.

However, the drying process remains an unresolved challenge.

Before introducing the activity into a workshop, I'd like to develop a more reliable method of preparing and drying the material.

For now, I see this as a promising first experiment rather than a completed workshop proposal.

What began as an attempt to repurpose an ordinary egg carton has opened up possibilities for exploring sculpture, recycling and tactile creativity with people of different ages and abilities.

Most importantly, it demonstrates how a participant's curiosity can become the starting point for an entirely new creative activity.
