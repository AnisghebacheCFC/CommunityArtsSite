Exploring Creative Expression Across Different Age Groups

By Anna Verdes

This Thursday and Friday, I had the opportunity to work with some of the older children at the nursery, rather than spending the whole week in the baby room.

Most of the children were around three years old, although there were a few younger and older children as well.

It was quite interesting going from working with babies to working with children who are a bit more independent, especially when it comes to the types of creative activities you can do with them.

Dancing and Rehearsing for the Nativity

On Thursday, we started the day with a little dance party. We did the Cha Cha Slide and then went on to rehearse some songs for the children's Christmas Nativity play.

Because it was my first day in that room, I was initially just following the room leader and getting to know how she did things.

But I got on really well with the children and eventually ended up leading some activities myself, which was really nice!

Something I thought about during the rehearsals was how theatre fits into expressive arts and design.

I think when people talk about creative activities in nurseries, they tend to think about painting, drawing or making things. But singing, dancing and performing are also different ways for children to express themselves.

Obviously, they're still quite young, but they're learning to remember songs, coordinate their movements and do things together as a group.

They're also working towards putting on a little performance for their parents, which I think is quite exciting for them.

The Differences Between Working With Babies and Older Children

One of the biggest differences I noticed was how much more you can communicate verbally with the older children.

They have more developed language and motor skills, so there's a wider range of things you can do with them.

You can give them instructions, teach them songs and encourage them to do certain things independently, like cleaning up after themselves.

That's not to say you can't start teaching babies these things as well.

I think it's important to start introducing simple instructions quite early on and then gradually build on them as the children get older and develop a better understanding of what you're asking them to do.

It's the same with creative activities. You have to think about what the children are capable of doing at that particular stage of their development.

Decorating a Christmas Tree

On Friday, I was back in the baby room.

There was a Christmas tree that had been made out of a triangular piece of paper, which the children had previously painted using their hands.

One of the practitioners gave the children stickers to decorate the tree with, and I think the idea was to eventually hang it up somewhere in the room.

It's quite a simple activity, but there's actually quite a bit involved in it for a baby.

Even something like picking up a sticker and placing it onto a piece of paper requires a level of coordination that they're still developing.

I also think it's nice when the children get to contribute to something collectively, rather than everyone necessarily having to make their own individual piece of artwork.

Spending Time in the Sensory Room

Probably the highlight of my Friday was getting to use the sensory room with some of the babies.

We'd joined up with another group of children who were slightly older than the ones in the baby room, and things started getting a little bit chaotic.

There was also a child who had a tendency to bite other children, so I had to think quite quickly about what I could do to prevent that from happening.

I spoke to the deputy manager, who helped me set up the sensory room, and I ended up taking three of the babies in there.

The sensory room is quite dark, and there are these carpets that you can switch on that have little illuminated stars in them.

By taking the children into a smaller and quieter environment, I was able to help the child settle down whilst also reducing the risk of the other children getting hurt.

I think sometimes when children are displaying certain behaviours, it's important to consider what's happening around them.

Rather than just telling a child not to do something, you can also think about whether there's something you can change about their environment.

There might be too much going on around them, or they might benefit from being in a smaller group for a little while.

Thinking About the Spaces We Create for Children

Something I've been thinking about is how much the environment itself can contribute to a child's experience.

In the sensory room, you're not necessarily giving the children something to make or asking them to complete an activity.

Sometimes it's just about giving them a different space to explore, with different lighting, textures and things to look at.

And I think there's something quite interesting about that from a creative perspective as well.

Throughout this week, I've been able to see quite a few different ways in which children engage with expressive arts and design, whether that's through dancing, performing, painting or exploring a sensory environment.

I think what's important is recognising that children are going to get different things out of these experiences depending on their age, their abilities and even how they're feeling on that particular day.

It's about being able to adapt to them rather than always expecting them to adapt to what you've planned.
