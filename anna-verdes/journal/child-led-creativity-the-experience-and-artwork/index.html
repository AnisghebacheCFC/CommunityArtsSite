Child-Led Creativity: The Experience and Artwork

By Anna Verdes

I've been working in the baby room at a nursery this week, and I've been thinking quite a bit about the activities we do with the children and what they're actually getting out of them.

When you're working with babies, they're all at different stages of development. Some are eating solid foods, some are still having purées, and others are only having milk. So you have to think about what each child is capable of doing and how you can adapt things for them.

A lot of the day involves playing with toys, singing, reading and doing different activities. Sometimes they're quite simple, but I think there's still a lot you can get out of them.

Exploring the Mirror

One activity I did was getting the babies to explore a mirror.

I was asking them things like, "Who's that in the mirror?" and talking about the different parts of their faces.

They found it funny! They were tapping on the mirror, looking at themselves and getting excited about what they could see.

It's quite a simple activity, but you're encouraging them to interact, recognise different features and develop their social skills.

I think sometimes people expect activities to be really elaborate, but actually, something as simple as looking in a mirror can be a good developmental opportunity.

When Activities Become More About the Finished Product

There was another activity involving footprints that I wasn't particularly keen on.

Basically, the idea was to paint the babies' feet and press them onto paper to make footprints for their parents.

I understand why people do these activities. It's nice for parents to receive something their child has made, and I can see the intention behind it.

But what I didn't like was that some of the babies were getting upset, and the activity was still being carried out.

For me, that's where I start questioning things.

In a nursery, activities should be child-led. Obviously, because they're babies, they need support and guidance, but that doesn't mean we should be deciding everything for them.

If a baby is crying or doesn't want to participate, I don't think we should be continuing just because we want to produce something for their parents.

It's almost like a conveyor belt. You're taking one baby, putting paint on their feet, pressing them onto paper, and then moving on to the next one.

I don't think that's what art should be about, especially at that age.

The babies aren't necessarily interested in making a footprint. They might be more interested in the feeling of the paint, the colour, or just exploring what's happening around them.

And I think that's what we should be focusing on.

How I Would Approach It Differently

If I were doing a printing activity, I would probably approach it using fruit instead.

Obviously, you'd have to make sure the children aren't allergic to anything and that whatever you're using is appropriate for their age.

But I think it could be a really interesting sensory activity.

I'd start by just letting the babies play with the fruit.

They could squash it, touch it, explore the textures and see what happens. Because it's food, they could also taste it if it's appropriate for them.

Then, once they'd had the opportunity to explore it themselves, I'd show them how they could use it to make prints on paper.

So rather than immediately telling them what they're supposed to make, you're letting them discover the material first and then introducing another way of using it.

Some babies might be interested in making prints, and others might just want to carry on exploring the fruit.

And that's fine.

I don't think the activity has to result in something that looks nice. The important thing is that they're getting something out of it developmentally.

You can still take photographs and document what they've been doing for the parents, but the focus would be on the children's experience rather than having a finished piece of artwork.

But Would That Encourage Children to Play With Their Food?

Something else I thought about was whether using fruit in an activity like this might encourage babies to play with their food during mealtimes.

I can understand why someone might think that, but I think they're two different things.

You have to remember that they're babies. They're still learning what food is, how to eat and what happens when they eat.

Even when they're having lunch, they're exploring. They're learning about different textures and tastes, and they're beginning to understand things like feeling hungry and then feeling full.

So I don't necessarily think using food for sensory play is a bad thing.

During mealtimes, you can encourage them to use their spoons, eat properly and understand the routine.

But during an activity, you're giving them an opportunity to explore something in a different way.

If anything, I think it could help them begin to understand that there are different situations where we do different things.

Obviously, it's something you'd have to think about when planning the activity, but I wouldn't dismiss the idea just because it involves food.

Thinking About Why We Do Activities

We also did other things throughout the day, like building with blocks, singing and reading.

They're all quite ordinary nursery activities, but I think what's important is how you approach them.

I don't think every activity needs to be complicated, and I definitely don't think everything needs to produce something that parents can take home.

Sometimes the children might just be exploring a texture, looking at themselves in a mirror or discovering that they can make a mark on paper.

That might not seem like much to an adult, but for a baby, it's an opportunity to learn something new.

I think it's important to question why we're doing certain activities rather than just doing them because they're familiar or because they'll produce something nice.

For me, it's not really about whether the artwork looks good.

It's about whether the children were actually able to explore, participate and enjoy what they were doing.
