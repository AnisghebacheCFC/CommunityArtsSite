Experimenting with Training Frequency

By Anis Ghebache

Revisiting High-Frequency Training

Over the years, I've experimented with various approaches to strength training, ranging from conventional programmes with designated rest days to more frequent sessions involving the same muscle groups.

One of my more successful experiments involved training the same muscles daily, using relatively heavy weights and low repetitions. Within a matter of months, I was able to curl 30 kilograms for six repetitions consistently, something I'm not currently capable of reproducing.

I also perceived considerable improvements in my tendon strength and general physical resilience during this period, although I recognise that these adaptations are difficult to isolate from improvements in muscular strength and technique.

Something I've noticed about myself is that extended periods without physical stimulation tend to leave me feeling sluggish. Even after a single day of complete rest, I sometimes find that my strength and explosiveness feel diminished.

Whether this is a consequence of reduced muscular activation, changes in my perception of exertion or simply becoming accustomed to daily exercise is something I'm interested in exploring further.

Introducing Exercise Throughout the Day

I've decided to experiment with a slightly different approach, retaining my heavier strength-training sessions every other day, incorporating yoga and cardiovascular exercise between them, and introducing smaller bouts of resistance training throughout my daily routine.

The idea is fairly straightforward. Whenever I enter one room, I perform bicep curls and overhead presses with a dumbbell. In another room, I complete pull-ups and hanging abdominal exercises.

These exercises are performed close to muscular failure, although I avoid pushing beyond what I can comfortably control.

My intention is to increase my overall training volume without necessarily extending the duration of my conventional gym sessions. By distributing the exercises throughout the day, I'm hoping to accumulate more work while allowing sufficient time between individual efforts.

There's also the question of circulation. Muscular contractions increase blood flow to the working tissues, and I suspect that regularly engaging my muscles throughout the day might help me feel warmer and less sedentary.

Whether this provides any meaningful recovery advantage remains to be seen. The additional exercises are still training, and therefore introduce their own recovery demands.

Assisted Pull-Ups and Changing Perspectives

During my gym session, I completed my usual pulling exercises, including rows, before moving on to pull-ups.

Although I'd already worked the relevant muscles quite extensively, I still felt reasonably fresh and was particularly enthusiastic about completing some additional work.

I decided to experiment with a form of mechanical drop set, beginning with less assistance and progressively introducing thicker resistance bands as I became fatigued.

There were three younger people nearby who were also performing pull-ups. Watching them reminded me of my own experiences when I was younger and considerably lighter.

At approximately 95 kilograms, pull-ups require a substantial amount of effort, even though I'm stronger in absolute terms than I was several years ago. It's an interesting reminder that relative strength and absolute strength aren't necessarily interchangeable.

I also found myself wondering what the others thought of my approach. There was a moment when I interpreted their behaviour as amusement or curiosity towards the resistance bands.

On reflection, however, I think that was largely projection on my part. Nobody actually criticised my technique, and there's little reason to assume they were particularly concerned with what I was doing.

Reflections

What interests me about this new routine is the possibility of increasing training frequency without abandoning the heavier sessions that I enjoy.

I have some confidence in the approach because of my previous experiences, although I'm conscious that past success doesn't necessarily establish why something worked.

For now, I'm interested in observing whether distributing additional volume throughout the day improves my performance, or whether the accumulated fatigue eventually begins to interfere with it.

It's an experiment I intend to pursue consistently, while remaining willing to revise my assumptions.
