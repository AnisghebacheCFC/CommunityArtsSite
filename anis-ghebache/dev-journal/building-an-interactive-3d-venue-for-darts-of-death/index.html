Building an Interactive 3D Venue for Darts of Death

By Anis Ghebache

Following my visit to Craft Metropolis in Brixton on 3 November, I began translating the measurements I'd collected into an interactive 3D model of the venue.

The purpose was to provide the Darts of Death organisers with an accessible digital representation of the space ahead of the upcoming darts tournament and social event on 4 December.

Rather than creating a highly detailed architectural visualisation, I wanted to produce something lightweight, functional and immediately useful. The model needed to communicate the venue's proportions and layout while remaining easy to access through a web browser.

Using Blender for the modelling and Three.js for the online viewer, I was able to put together a relatively simple but effective solution.

Reconstructing the Venue in Blender

The modelling process was straightforward.

Having already collected measurements of the venue's walls, stairs, railings, windows and other architectural features, I could use those dimensions to reconstruct the environment using basic geometric primitives.

Most of the scene consists of relatively simple objects positioned and scaled according to my calculations.

I wasn't particularly concerned with reproducing every architectural detail. The priority was to establish an accurate representation of the venue's proportions and the relationships between its different features.

This approach made the modelling process considerably more efficient.

There were a few details that required some approximation. For example, although I'd measured the railings during my visit, I hadn't recorded their height in my notebook.

Fortunately, this wasn't a particularly significant obstacle. The height could easily be confirmed with the venue at a later date, and it wasn't essential to the initial functionality of the model.

Similarly, I positioned the dartboard using photographs I'd taken during my visit, alongside reference images available online. Although I hadn't recorded its exact distance from the surrounding walls, these visual references were sufficient to place it convincingly.

I deliberately kept the dartboard low-poly, as its primary purpose was to communicate where it was situated within the venue.

Simplifying Textures for Browser Performance

One of my main considerations was keeping the model lightweight enough to run efficiently in a web browser.

To achieve this, I used simple materials and textures wherever possible.

For example, the floor tiles were created using a procedural texture, while the metal railings were represented with a straightforward texture applied across simplified geometry.

The real railings are considerably more complex, but reproducing that complexity would have added unnecessary detail to a model primarily intended for spatial planning.

I applied a similar approach to the venue's brick arch, using a repeating brick texture to suggest the original material.

Although the texture is visibly repetitive when examined closely, that limitation isn't particularly important in this context.

The model isn't intended to be a photorealistic reconstruction. It's a practical visual reference that allows people to understand the space and refresh their memory of its arrangement.

I also recreated the projection area on the back wall using a simple plane with the Darts of Death logo.

These visual details help make the environment recognisable without significantly increasing its complexity.

Implementing Back-Face Culling

One particularly useful feature was the implementation of back-face culling.

Because the venue is an enclosed interior, I wanted users to be able to examine the space from different angles without the external walls constantly obstructing their view.

By configuring the relevant geometry and materials in Blender, I could ensure that the back-facing surfaces wouldn't be rendered in the browser.

This allows the viewer to look through certain surfaces from outside the model while retaining the visible interior-facing geometry.

It's a relatively simple technique, but one that makes a considerable difference when navigating a compact architectural environment.

Having previously worked with exporting lightweight 3D assets for web applications, I was already familiar with the considerations involved in preparing models for Three.js.

This experience meant I could make decisions about geometry, textures and rendering behaviour during the modelling process rather than having to optimise everything afterwards.

Integrating the Model with Three.js

Once the model was prepared, I integrated it into my website using Three.js.

The process was relatively uncomplicated, largely because I'd deliberately maintained a lightweight structure throughout development.

The simple geometry and materials translated well into the browser-based environment, allowing users to navigate and inspect the model without needing to install Blender or any other specialist software.

This accessibility was central to the project.

I wanted the organisers to be able to open a link and immediately explore the venue, whether they were discussing the layout together or reviewing potential arrangements independently.

The viewer provides a shared spatial reference, making it easier to communicate ideas about furniture placement, circulation and other organisational considerations.

It also means the model can be revisited whenever necessary rather than existing solely as a resource for a single planning meeting.

Prioritising Functionality Over Detail

There were several additional features I considered implementing, including markers identifying the locations of electrical sockets.

Although I'd recorded these positions during my initial visit, I decided not to incorporate them into the first version.

This wasn't an oversight. It was a deliberate decision to prioritise getting a usable model into the hands of the organisers as quickly as possible.

The electrical socket locations could become useful when discussing cable routing and equipment placement, but they weren't essential to the model's immediate purpose.

Similarly, I haven't yet implemented the ability to move furniture or rearrange the venue directly within the viewer.

That remains a potential development for the future.

My intention is to introduce additional functionality according to demand rather than spending time developing features that the organisers may not actually need.

If the team finds that moving tables, positioning equipment or visualising different configurations would be useful, those tools can be incorporated later.

For now, the model successfully fulfils its primary objective: making the venue easier to visualise and discuss.

Adding an Easter Egg

Although the model was designed as a practical planning resource, I couldn't resist introducing something a little more playful.

I added a hidden Easter egg connected to the Darts of Death logo.

When users interact with the logo, they discover a video of the event organiser referencing an ongoing joke within the group.

It was a small addition, but one that gave the model a little personality.

When I shared the viewer in the organisers' WhatsApp group, I offered to buy a drink for whoever managed to discover the Easter egg.

The response was amusing, and people seemed to enjoy the surprise.

I particularly liked this aspect of the project because it introduced an element of play into something that could otherwise have been a purely functional technical exercise.

Darts of Death is ultimately a social event centred around creativity, humour and shared experiences, so including a little inside joke felt appropriate.

Sharing the Model with the Organisers

The response from the organisers was overwhelmingly positive.

They were delighted with the model and considered it an excellent idea, particularly because of its potential usefulness beyond the upcoming event.

The organising team already has an established working relationship with Craft Metropolis, and the venue has been supportive of their events, offering favourable arrangements and reduced pricing.

This makes it quite likely that the space will be used again.

Consequently, the 3D model isn't necessarily a resource with a single use.

It can continue to support future events, allowing the organisers to revisit the venue's layout and experiment with different arrangements without having to reconstruct their understanding of the space each time.

That longer-term usefulness was particularly satisfying.

Something relatively straightforward to create could continue providing value well beyond the immediate production.

Reflections on the Process

What I enjoyed most about this project was how naturally the different stages came together.

The modelling itself wasn't technically demanding. It involved positioning simple geometric objects according to measurements I'd already collected.

Similarly, integrating the model into Three.js was straightforward because I'd designed the assets with browser performance in mind from the beginning.

The value of the project wasn't necessarily in its technical complexity, but in recognising an opportunity to apply existing skills to a practical problem.

By dedicating some time to documenting and reconstructing the venue, I was able to create a resource that could make the planning process easier for everyone involved.

I also found it rewarding to see how enthusiastically the organisers received it.

Their response reinforced something I'd discussed with the organiser months earlier: the potential of a creative community when its members contribute their individual skills towards a shared experience.

I wasn't formally assigned the task of producing a digital venue model. It was something I volunteered to do because I felt it could be useful.

The fact that it was appreciated, and that it may continue to serve future events, made the effort particularly worthwhile.

Looking Ahead

The current version of the model provides a functional, accessible representation of Craft Metropolis that can be explored directly through a web browser.

There are several possible directions for further development, including interactive furniture placement, electrical socket markers and additional tools for planning equipment layouts.

However, I intend to let the organisers' needs determine which features are worth implementing.

There's little value in introducing complexity for its own sake.

For now, I'm pleased that the model is already serving its intended purpose and has been received so positively.

More broadly, this project has reinforced my interest in using 3D technology to support collaborative creative processes.

Not every 3D project needs to be visually elaborate or technically ambitious to have a meaningful impact.

Sometimes, the most effective application of a skill is simply making something useful, accessible and enjoyable for the people around you.
