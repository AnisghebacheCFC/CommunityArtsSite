Peer Mentorship, Creative Identity and New Connections

By Anis Ghebache

The Peer Mentor Kick-Off

On Tuesday 27 October, I attended the Postgraduate Peer Mentorship Programme's kick-off meeting at London College of Communication.

Earlier that afternoon, I'd met with Louise to catch up on some of the training material I'd missed during our first session.

The kick-off provided an opportunity to meet other mentors and students, learn more about the programme and begin making connections.

I particularly enjoyed the opportunity to engage in informal conversations with people from different creative backgrounds.

Having recently completed my postgraduate studies, I was interested in hearing how current students were approaching their time at university, particularly those beginning their first year.

A Conversation About Creative Identity

During the kick-off, I had a particularly enjoyable conversation with a first-year student from London who was interested in visual storytelling.

She spoke about some of her creative interests and experiences, including documentary work connected to a community centre.

I was pleased to hear that she was already engaging with creative opportunities outside her university studies.

Our discussion eventually turned towards the value of creative work, particularly when considering opportunities that don't necessarily provide financial compensation.

I encouraged her to recognise the value of her contributions and to be conscious of how she presented herself professionally.

Although I believe unpaid projects can provide meaningful experiences, particularly when their objectives align with our own interests, I think there's an important distinction between choosing to contribute voluntarily and developing a reputation as someone whose work is freely available.

I wanted to communicate that her creative abilities deserved recognition, but also that her personality, experiences and individual perspective were valuable in their own right.

I've increasingly come to appreciate that a person's identity can contribute just as much to their creative practice as their technical abilities.

The way somebody communicates, approaches problems and engages with others can distinguish their work and influence the opportunities they encounter.

Rather than attempting to construct an artificial professional identity, I think there's value in recognising and developing the qualities that already make someone distinctive.

Finding Direction While Remaining Open to Change

We also spoke about how she might approach her three years at university.

I encouraged her to think about what she hoped to achieve during that time and to pursue opportunities that would contribute towards those ambitions.

However, I was conscious of not wanting to become overly prescriptive.

Having experienced how much my own interests have evolved, I recognise that some of the most meaningful developments in my practice have emerged unexpectedly.

A rigid commitment to an initial plan might provide direction, but it can also prevent somebody from recognising opportunities that fall outside their original expectations.

I therefore wanted to encourage a balance between intentionality and openness.

It's useful to have ambitions and goals, but I think it's equally important to allow those ambitions to change as our experiences develop.

In many ways, this reflects my own approach to creative practice.

My interests have gradually expanded beyond animation into creative technology, filmmaking, education and community engagement.

Many of these developments emerged through conversations, spontaneous decisions and opportunities that I hadn't anticipated.

I wouldn't want to discourage someone from exploring similar possibilities by presenting my own experiences as a definitive model for success.

Reflecting on My Approach to Mentorship

One thing I noticed during our conversation was how naturally I began offering advice.

I hadn't prepared anything in advance. My responses emerged through listening to her experiences and considering whether my perspective might be useful.

Although I enjoyed the exchange, it also made me conscious of the distinction between offering guidance and attempting to direct somebody else's development.

I don't want mentorship to become an exercise in projecting my own ambitions or experiences onto another person.

Instead, I hope to create opportunities for students to discuss their ideas, consider different perspectives and develop greater confidence in their own judgement.

Sometimes this might involve offering practical information or sharing relevant experiences.

At other times, it might simply mean listening and providing someone with the space to articulate their thoughts.

I find the idea of mentorship as a reciprocal exchange particularly appealing.

Although one person may have greater experience within a particular environment, both people can learn something from the interaction.

Even during this relatively brief conversation, I found myself reconsidering aspects of my own approach to creative work.

The Introduction Events

Following the kick-off, I attended the two introductory events scheduled for Thursday 29 October and Monday 2 November.

These gatherings were intended to provide further opportunities for mentors and mentees to become acquainted.

The first introduction took place at LCC, beginning in Lecture Theatre C before moving into a more informal setting.

I had the opportunity to speak with some of the other mentors, including Shannon, who had been in the same university year group as me, although studying a different course.

Shannon had participated in the mentorship programme the previous year, making this her second year as a mentor.

It was lovely to see her again and have a brief catch-up, although we didn't have particularly long to talk.

The second introduction, held on Monday 2 November, was considerably quieter.

Attendance was relatively low, and there were limited opportunities for me to interact with students.

Nevertheless, I appreciated the chance to speak with other mentors and become more familiar with some of the people involved in the programme.

Hoping to Connect with Animation Students

One thing that remains unresolved is establishing contact with the students from my own discipline whom I'm hoping to support.

Although I've attended the training, kick-off and introduction events, I haven't yet had much opportunity to develop meaningful connections with students from the animation course.

This is something I'm particularly interested in pursuing.

Having recently completed my MA in 3D Computer Animation, I feel that my experiences are still relatively fresh.

I can remember the challenges of navigating the course, managing creative projects and trying to establish a direction for my practice.

I'd like to make those experiences available to students who might find them useful.

Earlier in the programme, I'd also taken the opportunity to introduce the mentorship scheme to a classroom of new animation students.

Louise subsequently mentioned that approximately three students from the classroom had expressed interest, which I found encouraging.

However, I haven't yet had an opportunity to establish the kind of ongoing mentoring relationship I'd originally anticipated.

For now, I'm hoping that the next stage of the programme will provide more opportunities to connect directly with students and begin developing those relationships.

Reflections on the Experience

Looking back on these events, I think the conversation during the kick-off was the most meaningful part of the experience for me.

It demonstrated how naturally an informal interaction can develop into a discussion about personal ambitions, professional identity and creative development.

I also enjoyed meeting other mentors and reconnecting with Shannon.

Although the gatherings themselves were relatively modest, they provided an introduction to some of the people involved in the programme.

My experiences with Community Arts have increasingly reinforced my appreciation for the opportunities that emerge when people are brought together and encouraged to exchange ideas.

I'm hoping that mentorship will offer another structure through which I can contribute to this process.

I don't necessarily believe that successful mentorship involves providing an answer to every question.

Instead, I'd like to offer students an opportunity to consider their ideas from another perspective, recognise the value of their individual experiences and become more confident in pursuing their own ambitions.

If my involvement can contribute towards that, even in a relatively small way, then I'll consider it worthwhile.

A Small Observation

Following the introduction event on Thursday 29 October, I went for a walk and passed The Rosy Hue, a restaurant and bar not far from LCC.

Outside was a small stone statue of a dog that somebody had dressed in a witch's hat for the approaching Halloween celebrations.

I found the gesture absolutely delightful.

However, I also noticed that the dog's nose was broken.

I began thinking about the contrasting ways people interact with their surroundings.

Somebody had taken the time to decorate this otherwise ordinary statue, transforming it into something playful and endearing, while at some point its face had also been damaged.

Although I couldn't know exactly how the damage had occurred, I found myself imagining the different attitudes that might have contributed to its appearance.

One interaction seemed to express affection and playfulness, while the other suggested a possible disregard for the object.

There was something particularly sad about seeing the broken face of such an innocent-looking little dog.

Why would anyone want to damage something like that?

I suppose what interested me was how the same object could inspire such different responses.

The statue itself hadn't changed its purpose or meaning, yet the people encountering it had left very different marks upon it.

It's a small observation, but one that made me think about how our individual actions, however insignificant they might seem, contribute to the character of the environments we share.
