Giving the Tower Some Ground and Breathing Room

By Anis Ghebache

A Grassy Mound and a Tree

Giving the Tower Some Ground

I’d been looking at the tower on its own for quite a while. That was useful when I needed to see how the bricks fitted together, but I was starting to find it difficult to imagine the tower existing anywhere beyond the 3D scene I’d built for it.

I put it on a small grassy mound. As soon as it had something to stand on, I began looking at it differently. I could see where its weight might sit and imagine looking up at it from the ground.

Working With the Shape of the Ground

I liked the idea of an uneven piece of ground underneath such a regular structure. The bricks line up around a circle and rise in layers. The grass follows a softer outline around the bottom.

It gives me a way to think about the camera too. A view of the tower can now tell you something about where you’re standing in relation to the ground. You can see more of the mound from one angle and more of the tower’s height from another.

Adding a Tree

The tree gave me something familiar to judge the tower against. Even a simple tree has a recognisable scale. There’s a trunk, a point where the branches begin and a sense of how far they spread.

I also liked what it did to the outline of the scene. The tree brings in some of the irregular shapes I’ve enjoyed looking at when working with plants in our workshops. Once I put the tree and tower together, I could see how much the space between them mattered. A branch can lead your eye towards the tower, but it can also end up exactly where a line of text needs to sit.

Making the Tree Easier to Change

I’m exploring a procedural approach so I can adjust the tree’s shape and detail as I work. I’d like to be able to change how far the branches reach and how dense the canopy feels as I try the composition on different screens.

I’ve also been looking at level of detail, a technique used in 3D rendering to show simpler geometry when an object is farther from the camera. On a phone, I’m interested in which details I can simplify while keeping the outline of the canopy recognisable. The tree has to share the scene with a tower somebody can spin, so I want to judge how much detail it can carry while the rotation still feels responsive.

Thinking About the Seasons

I’d like to see what happens if the tree changes through the year. The same tower could feel quite different beside a bare winter canopy or a tree with new leaves. It would give the scene another way to show that time has passed.

I’m also thinking about what could come from the workshops. We already spend time noticing the shapes of plants, using leaves to make prints and working with different colours. I’d be interested to see whether something made during one of those sessions could find its way into the landscape around the tower.

Giving the Tower Some Breathing Room

Dropping the Text

I dropped the text lower so it stopped fighting the tree. “Tap on a brick” had been sitting over the branches, and the brown lettering was difficult to read over parts of the canopy.

When I looked at the whole scene, I realised there was a space underneath the text that I wasn’t making much use of. Moving it down gave the words a clearer place to sit and let me see the outline of the tree again.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines give me a way to check that decision. For regular-sized text, Level AA calls for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1. With a background that varies across the scene, I need to look at what sits directly behind the letters. Moving the prompt clear of the branches gave me a more consistent background to work with.

Looking at the Space Around It

I wrote about Gestalt principles as part of my undergraduate dissertation. They played a big role in my artistic development when I was working as a graphic designer. I started paying more attention to what the space between things was telling me. Moving a line of text a little closer to an image could change which image I thought it belonged to.

That came back to me when I was looking at the tree. Nielsen Norman Group describes proximity as the tendency to see things that sit close together as part of the same group. I’d placed the prompt right over the branches, so it looked like it belonged to the tree, even though the instruction was about the bricks. Moving the words down gave the tree its outline back and made it easier to read the prompt as part of the tower interaction.

I used to think about this on a flat page. Here the branches have depth and the tower can turn, but I’m still asking a familiar question. When somebody looks at the scene for the first time, what do they think goes together?

“Tap” and “Click”

“Tap on a brick” makes sense on a phone. On a desktop, I’d usually say “click.” On a tablet or a touchscreen laptop, either word might be appropriate depending on how somebody is using it.

I could change the instruction according to the device or orientation. That would get me closer in the obvious cases, but I’d still be making assumptions about how somebody is using the screen. It made me wonder whether the tower could show somebody where to start.

Trying to Show the Interaction

I’ve been thinking about letting one brick light up briefly, then another somewhere else. That might be enough to make somebody curious about what happens when they touch the tower. It could also give visitors who don’t read English a way to discover the interaction.

In their visual search experiments, psychologists Steven Yantis and John Jonides found that the sudden appearance of an item could draw attention to it. That gives me a reason to try a local change on the tower. The question for me is whether somebody sees the brick light up and understands that they can touch it. I’d have to watch somebody use the feature to find that out.

I considered a ripple that would spread across the bricks. My eye followed the ripple and quickly lost the brick that started it. It felt a bit too much like a game effect. I’d like to see what happens when one brick lights up and the rest of the tower stays quiet.

Seeing How Much the Page Needs to Say

For now, the prompt sits lower and is easier to read. I’d like to try a version with the brief glow and show both to somebody who hasn’t seen the tower before. Do they turn it straight away? Do they touch the brick that lights up? Do they read the prompt while they’re already reaching for the screen?

I’ve spent long enough with the tower that I already know where to touch. I want to see where somebody else looks first.
