Recovery, Restraint and Movement

By Anis Ghebache

The Aftermath of Moving Furniture

I woke up on Monday experiencing some discomfort in my lower back, which I attributed to the previous day's furniture moving.

One particularly demanding task had involved manoeuvring a large pull-out sofa up a narrow staircase.

The sofa was awkwardly shaped, with an uneven distribution of weight. Depending on how it was carried, the pull-out mechanism would begin to open, requiring us to apply additional force simply to keep the structure together.

This meant engaging the chest, shoulders and back while simultaneously supporting and transporting the object.

At one point, the staircase became so narrow that I couldn't fit between the sofa and the wall.

I ended up positioning myself underneath it and pushing upwards to reduce the load being carried by the others.

The movement was somewhat reminiscent of a squat, although the object itself was considerably less stable than a barbell, and the overall weight was shared between several people.

I was mindful of my positioning throughout, but the sustained effort evidently placed a considerable demand on my lower back.

Choosing Not to Train

Monday would ordinarily have been a chest and triceps session.

Although I would have preferred to complete my usual workout, I was conscious that even exercises which don't directly target the lower back can place considerable demands on it.

Moving dumbbells into position, racking weights and transitioning between exercises all involve some degree of trunk stabilisation.

Given the discomfort I was experiencing, I decided that completing a heavy session would introduce unnecessary strain.

This was somewhat frustrating, particularly because I've been enjoying the increased frequency of my training.

However, I was also conscious that aggravating the discomfort could interfere with subsequent sessions.

Maintaining Activity

Rather than remaining completely inactive, I decided to maintain a reasonable amount of movement throughout the day.

I walked frequently and avoided sitting in one position for extended periods, as I was concerned that prolonged inactivity might leave my lower back feeling increasingly stiff.

I also completed some smaller isolation exercises, including bicep curls and supported overhead pressing with my dumbbell, while avoiding movements that noticeably aggravated the affected area.

My intention was to maintain some muscular activity and circulation without placing substantial additional demands on my lower back.

I experienced a modest improvement throughout the day, although I wouldn't necessarily attribute this exclusively to the exercises.

Reflections

Something I've been considering is the relationship between functional strength and the ability to tolerate awkward, sustained loads.

The previous day's experience reinforced my interest in farmer's carries and other exercises involving loaded movement.

At the same time, Monday demonstrated that being capable of completing a physically demanding task doesn't necessarily mean that the body is immediately prepared for further heavy training.

I think there's an important distinction between maintaining consistency and insisting upon completing a particular workout regardless of the circumstances.

For someone who enjoys training frequently, voluntarily reducing the intensity of a session can be surprisingly difficult.

Nevertheless, I was satisfied with my decision to prioritise recovery rather than risk prolonging the discomfort.

The objective, after all, is to maintain a sustainable training practice rather than maximise the amount of work completed on any particular day.
