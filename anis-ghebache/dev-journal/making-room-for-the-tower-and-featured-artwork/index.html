Making Room for the Tower and Featured Artwork

By Anis Ghebache


Making the Tower Work on a Phone

I was using the site on my phone when I realised the tower was taking over the scroll gesture. I’d put my thumb down somewhere in the scene and swipe up to get to the rest of the website, but the page would stay where it was. On a desktop I could move the mouse away or use the scroll wheel. On a phone, the feature fills most of the screen, so it’s where my thumb naturally lands.

I changed it so dragging sideways turns the tower and swiping up or down scrolls the page, even if the gesture starts over the bricks. I can turn the tower a little, tap a brick and then carry on down the website from the same place. The glow helps me see which brick I’ve selected once I lift my thumb.

I also adjusted the camera for portrait and landscape. On a wider screen, the tower and tree have more room beside one another. In portrait, everything sits much closer together. I used the rule of thirds as a rough guide while I worked out how much ground to show and how much space to give the tree and tower.

Working on the phone layout made me think about two moments in particular. The first is what you see when the feature opens. The second is what happens when you’re ready to move on. I wanted the featured artwork to be immediately visible and readable, and I wanted a normal upward swipe to take you straight to the rest of the site.

Making Room for Featured Artwork

The featured artwork sits in the top left of the scene. It’s one of the first things you see when the feature opens, alongside the tree and tower. I wanted to give one piece that space while the tower was still growing, so you could spend a moment with the work as soon as you arrived.

Readability was a key part of the placement. I chose the top left because it’s one of the first areas people tend to look at when scanning a page in English. Nielsen Norman Group’s eye-tracking research found that people scanning left-to-right text tend to give more attention to the top and left of a page. The pattern varies with the layout and the visitor’s task, but it gave me a reason to put the piece where it could be picked up early.

I initially put the “Featured Artwork” label in front of the tree. The branches made the words harder to read, and the label was some distance from the image it described. I moved it directly above the artwork and put the upload date underneath it. The dark text sits against the pale sky, where the background is much simpler. The date follows the piece, so it changes when a different work is featured.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines set a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for ordinary text at Level AA. That gives me a benchmark when I’m choosing where text sits in a scene with branches, grass and shadows. With the label and date together above the image, I can take in the information at a glance, spend some time with the artwork or turn the tower. When I’m ready to see the rest of the website, I can swipe straight through.
