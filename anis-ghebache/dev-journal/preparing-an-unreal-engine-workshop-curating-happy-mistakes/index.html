Preparing an Unreal Engine Workshop: Curating Happy Mistakes

By Anis Ghebache

Developing an Introduction to Unreal Engine

I've been preparing a 30-minute introductory lesson in Unreal Engine for Computer Games Arts students, exploring how I can introduce fundamental concepts in 3D environment development while creating opportunities for experimentation and problem-solving.

The lesson begins with a blank project and gradually introduces students to creating a playable 3D environment, covering viewport navigation, manipulating Actors, creating basic geometry, introducing a third-person character and experimenting with how that character interacts with the environment.

While planning the workshop, I've been thinking about how to make the demonstrations more engaging, particularly for students who might be encountering Unreal Engine for the first time. Rather than simply introducing tools and explaining their functionality, I've been looking for opportunities to demonstrate why certain workflows are useful and what happens when things don't work as expected.

Something I've become particularly interested in is the idea of curating happy mistakes.

Curating Happy Mistakes

My approach involves deliberately recreating common mistakes that students might encounter when learning unfamiliar software, incorporating them into a structured learning experience.

Rather than demonstrating an entirely polished workflow, I'm interested in approaching certain exercises from the perspective of a beginner, anticipating where they might encounter difficulties and creating opportunities to investigate those problems together.

One of the demonstrations I've prepared involves constructing a slope that the character is unable to climb.

Using Unreal's modelling tools, I can create an inclined surface and introduce a third-person character to test whether it's possible to navigate.

I've deliberately designed the slope to exceed the character's default Walkable Floor Angle of approximately 45 degrees, creating a problem that students can observe and investigate.

Rather than immediately explaining the solution, I intend to ask students to estimate the angle of the slope and consider why the character is unable to move across it.

By opening the character's Blueprint and increasing the Walkable Floor Angle to 60 degrees, we can test the environment again and observe how a relatively small adjustment changes the character's behaviour.

I like this exercise because it introduces students to Blueprints through something they can immediately observe. It also encourages them to think about the relationship between geometry, movement and gameplay, rather than treating these as entirely separate processes.

Experimenting with Static Meshes

Another exercise involves demonstrating the difference between constructing environments using individual primitive shapes and editing an existing Static Mesh.

I started by placing several cubes into the environment and experimenting with Unreal Engine's modelling tools, particularly Insert Edge Loop and Extrude, to create more complex geometry.

For this demonstration, I deliberately modified the underlying Static Mesh asset so that every other instance of the same mesh would change alongside it.

I wanted to recreate the kind of mistake a student might make when first experimenting with the modelling tools, particularly if they haven't yet understood the distinction between an object placed in a level and the asset it references.

Imagine modifying a single cube, only to discover that every other cube in the environment has changed as well.

It's a relatively simple problem, but one that naturally encourages questions about how objects and assets are organised within Unreal Engine.

From here, I can introduce the process of duplicating the mesh asset itself, allowing individual objects to be modified independently.

What interests me about this approach is that the mistake becomes part of the teaching process rather than something to be avoided.

By deliberately reproducing these situations, I can demonstrate not only how to resolve them, but why they happen in the first place.

Collision, Geometry and Character Movement

While developing the exercises, I also experimented with more complex geometry and how the character interacts with different surfaces.

One of my tests involved creating concave sections within a mesh and examining how Unreal Engine handles collision.

The character's feet weren't making appropriate contact with the geometry, with one appearing to float above the surface while the other remained planted.

Although the character's inverse kinematics were functioning correctly, the collision geometry wasn't accurately representing the surface.

Unreal Engine's automatically generated convex collision shapes can approximate complex geometry in ways that don't always correspond to the visible mesh, particularly around concave sections.

Changing the Static Mesh's Collision Complexity to Use Complex Collision As Simple allowed Unreal to calculate collisions using the mesh's actual triangles, resolving the issue.

This exercise became another opportunity to explore the relationship between different systems within Unreal Engine.

A student encountering the same problem might reasonably assume that something was wrong with the character's animation, when the underlying cause was actually related to the environment's collision geometry.

Although this particular demonstration is probably too advanced for a 30-minute introductory lesson, I found it useful to investigate how I could communicate the problem and its solution.

It could also form the basis of a more advanced workshop exploring collision, character movement and animation.

Teaching as an Extension of Creative Practice

One thing I've noticed throughout this preparation is how much teaching encourages me to investigate the tools I'm already using.

I'm familiar with working across 3D modelling, animation, compositing and interactive environments, but developing a workshop requires me to consider how these processes can be broken down and communicated to somebody else.

I've found myself revisiting relatively simple features and deliberately approaching them as somebody who might be encountering the software for the first time.

This has been particularly interesting because it requires me to anticipate not only what students need to learn, but also how they might misunderstand something.

Rather than waiting for these problems to emerge naturally, I'm exploring how I can introduce them at appropriate moments, allowing students to develop an understanding of the software through observation, experimentation and discussion.

My intention isn't to make mistakes on behalf of students or remove every possible obstacle from their learning. Instead, I'd like to demonstrate common difficulties in a controlled environment, allowing students to recognise these situations and develop the confidence to investigate similar problems independently.

I also think there's something valuable about making the process of learning feel more human. When demonstrating technical software, it's easy to focus entirely on the correct sequence of actions, but this doesn't necessarily reflect the experience of somebody learning it for the first time.

By introducing mistakes deliberately, I can create moments where an unexpected result becomes an opportunity to ask questions, rather than something that interrupts the lesson.

Of course, this also requires a thorough understanding of the software. To introduce a mistake intentionally, I need to understand what causes it, how it can be resolved and how it relates to the wider workflow.

I've found that this process encourages me to revisit my own knowledge and think more carefully about how different systems interact.

As I continue developing my work across animation, interactive media and community arts, I'm increasingly interested in finding opportunities to share the techniques and knowledge I've acquired.

Preparing this workshop has been another opportunity to explore the relationship between creative practice and education, particularly how technical experimentation can inform the way I design learning experiences.

What I find particularly rewarding is recognising how even the smallest technical decisions can become opportunities for teaching. Something as simple as modifying a cube, adjusting a slope or investigating a collision issue can introduce students to concepts that might otherwise seem quite abstract.

I'm interested in continuing to develop this approach, identifying common challenges within creative software and exploring how they can be transformed into accessible, engaging exercises.

Ultimately, I want to create learning experiences that encourage curiosity, independent thinking and experimentation, while giving students the practical knowledge they need to develop their own work.

For me, curating happy mistakes is one way of achieving that.
