Beyond Automation: AI as an Extension of Human Creativity

By Anis Ghebache

RETHINKING THE ROLE OF AI IN CREATIVE PRACTICE

At the AI Creative Summit, speakers discussed how artificial intelligence is moving into structured production pipelines, alongside increasingly important questions surrounding authorship, authenticity and trust.

What interested me most, however, was not simply how AI could accelerate production, but how it might expand the possibilities of artistic expression.

Much of the conversation surrounding generative AI focuses on efficiency. Faster workflows, reduced production costs and the automation of increasingly complex tasks. While these developments have practical value, I find myself more interested in a different question: what can we create with AI that we couldn't meaningfully create before?

For me, this distinction is important. Technology becomes particularly interesting when it enables new forms of creative thinking, rather than simply replacing existing methods of production.

MAKING CREATIVE THINKING VISIBLE

One of the most interesting possibilities lies in combining traditional artistic workflows with generative systems.

Rather than approaching AI as a tool that produces a finished image or sequence from a single instruction, I am interested in integrating it into a continuous process of development.

An artist might begin with a rough three-dimensional blockout, use generative tools to explore visual possibilities, and then select, modify and redevelop the results.

The process becomes iterative. Each output informs the next decision, allowing the artist to progressively discover and refine their intentions.

This is particularly relevant to my own creative practice because I experience aphantasia, meaning I cannot voluntarily visualise images in my mind.

When developing an idea, I rely heavily on external visual feedback. Three-dimensional modelling has always been particularly valuable to me for this reason. It provides a space in which I can construct, observe and manipulate ideas that I cannot otherwise visualise internally.

AI introduces another dimension to that process. By allowing visual possibilities to emerge quickly, it creates opportunities to evaluate directions that might otherwise require substantial time to produce.

I don't regard this as a replacement for imagination. Rather, it offers another means through which imagination can be expressed, examined and developed.

The resulting work remains dependent on the artist's judgement, curiosity and willingness to experiment.

FROM STATIC ARTWORKS TO RESPONSIVE EXPERIENCES

Another area I find particularly compelling is the possibility of creating artwork that responds dynamically to its audience.

This is something I previously explored as a potential direction for practice-based doctoral research, although I have not yet developed it into a sustained project.

Traditionally, an artwork is created and subsequently encountered by an audience. While interpretation varies between individuals, the work itself often remains relatively fixed.

What happens when the artwork can respond?

Imagine an environment that changes its composition, narrative or behaviour in response to the people experiencing it. Rather than presenting an entirely predetermined sequence, the artist establishes a system capable of producing different outcomes through interaction.

Developments in real-time 3D technology, generative AI and agentic systems make this an increasingly interesting area to investigate.

The possibilities extend beyond entertainment or technical demonstration. Responsive environments could provide opportunities for participation, personal expression and collective creative experiences.

I am particularly interested in the relationship between artistic intention and audience agency. How much control should the artist retain? At what point does the audience become a collaborator? Can an artwork remain recognisably authored while allowing its form to evolve?

These are questions I hope to explore further through my creative practice.

AUTHORSHIP, AUTHENTICITY AND CRAFTSMANSHIP

Of course, expanding creative possibilities also introduces difficult questions.

If an artist generates an image using AI, where does authorship reside? Does the value of an artwork depend on the labour required to produce it, the decisions behind it, or the experience it creates?

I don't believe these questions have simple answers.

Traditional artistic processes involve accumulated knowledge, technical ability and craftsmanship. These qualities matter, not simply because they require time to develop, but because they shape the decisions an artist is capable of making.

The danger of relying too heavily on generative systems is that creative decisions can become increasingly disconnected from the artist.

If every decision is delegated, the artist risks becoming a spectator to the process rather than an active participant.

I therefore find approaches that preserve opportunities for intervention, experimentation and refinement more compelling than systems designed to eliminate those stages entirely.

An artist's relationship with the medium remains important, even when the medium itself changes.

Authenticity, in this context, is not necessarily determined by whether AI was used. It also concerns the transparency of the process, the intentionality behind the work and the extent to which the artist meaningfully contributes to its development.

TECHNOLOGY IN SERVICE OF HUMAN EXPRESSION

These considerations connect closely with the philosophy behind Community Arts, an initiative I established to create opportunities for artistic participation and meaningful social connection.

Through our workshops, we encourage people to engage directly with creative materials, explore unfamiliar processes and develop confidence through making.

The physical act of creating is valuable in itself. It provides opportunities for discovery, conversation and shared experience that extend beyond the finished artwork.

My interest in AI does not contradict this philosophy.

Rather, I see emerging technology as another potential extension of human expression, one that should be developed and evaluated according to its capacity to enrich human experiences.

An artistic process does not necessarily become more valuable because it incorporates sophisticated technology. Equally, technological involvement does not automatically diminish the value of an artwork.

What matters is what the process enables people to experience, understand or communicate.

This also means taking the ethical implications seriously.

Questions surrounding consent, intellectual property, training data, transparency and the treatment of working artists are fundamental to the responsible development of these systems.

Emerging approaches to content provenance, including C2PA, alongside mechanisms for artist protection and meaningful choices regarding the use of creative work in AI training, are important areas for further consideration.

If AI is to become a meaningful part of creative culture, its development must account for the people whose work and experiences contribute to that culture.

LOOKING AHEAD

As I begin studying AI for Film and Television at the National Film and Television School, I want to explore these questions through practical experimentation.

I am particularly interested in workflows that combine real-time 3D environments, generative systems and artist-directed processes.

I also want to investigate how these technologies might support interactive, responsive experiences without diminishing human intention or creative ownership.

This journal will provide a space to document that development, including experiments, technical discoveries, unsuccessful approaches and reflections on the wider implications of emerging technologies.

I don't expect every experiment to produce a successful outcome. The value of documenting the process lies partly in making those limitations visible.

Ultimately, my interest is not in using AI simply because it is new, or in treating automation as an inevitable measure of progress.

It is in understanding how emerging technologies might extend our capacity to imagine, create and connect.

The question I want to continue asking is:

What can I create with AI that I couldn't meaningfully create before?

And perhaps more importantly, how can those possibilities contribute to human expression, participation and connection?
