Preparing for Darts of Death: Measuring a Venue for 3D Spatial Planning

By Anis Ghebache

On 3 November, I visited Craft Metropolis, a craft beer shop and bar in Brixton, to take measurements of the venue ahead of the upcoming Darts of Death tournament and social event on 4 December.

The venue hosted last year's Halloween event, and although this year's edition was originally planned for Halloween, it was subsequently postponed.

With approximately a month until the event, I wanted to contribute something practical to the planning process.

My idea was to create an accurate 3D representation of the venue in Blender, which could eventually be viewed and manipulated online. This would allow the organising team to explore different spatial arrangements, understand the venue's constraints and make more informed decisions before physically setting anything up.

Getting Involved with Darts of Death

I've become friends with the organiser of Darts of Death, and one thing I particularly admire about him is his enthusiasm for creating experiences that bring people together.

He's someone who genuinely enjoys putting on a show and creating opportunities for people to collaborate, express themselves and share their enthusiasm with others.

A few months ago, we had a meaningful conversation about the nature of the event and the community surrounding it.

Darts of Death brings together a diverse group of creatives, many of whom possess skills that could contribute to the event in different ways.

We discussed the potential of drawing upon those talents more deliberately, creating an environment in which people could contribute their expertise while collectively improving the experience for everyone involved.

That conversation resonated with me.

I've since been added to the event's staff group chat and have contributed creative suggestions, although I'm not officially responsible for organising or designing the event.

Nevertheless, I wanted to take some initiative.

The organisers are talented people with busy schedules, and I felt that dedicating some of my own time to preparing useful resources could help make their work easier.

Why Measure the Venue?

The organising team had discussed arranging a meeting to plan the venue's layout and design.

I began thinking about what information would be useful during that meeting.

Although I could find basic dimensions of the venue online, including an approximate footprint of seven by fifteen metres, these measurements offered only a limited understanding of the space.

Craft Metropolis has a distinctive, irregular layout, with an upper level, railings, stairs and architectural features that complicate its usable floor area.

Simply knowing the approximate length and width of the building wouldn't be enough to understand how furniture, equipment and people could be accommodated.

I wanted to establish a much clearer picture of the venue's proportions.

With that in mind, I took a tape measure and visited the space to document its physical characteristics.

I measured the stairs, railings, tables, windows, wall spacing and the positions of electrical sockets, along with other structural details that could influence the event's layout.

The objective wasn't to make design decisions on behalf of the organisers.

Rather, I wanted to prepare a reliable spatial reference that would make their subsequent discussions more productive.

Documenting the Space

Taking measurements was a relatively straightforward process, although the venue's dimensions presented a few practical inconveniences.

The most awkward measurement was the overall width of the space.

My tape measure wasn't long enough to cover the entire distance in one go, so I had to divide it into smaller sections and measure them individually.

This required a little more care when recording dimensions, particularly where architectural features interrupted the space.

Otherwise, the visit was largely uneventful.

I was already familiar with the venue's appearance, and photographs available online had given me a reasonable understanding of its layout.

What I lacked was accurate information about its proportions.

This distinction is important because visual references can communicate the character of a space without necessarily revealing its practical constraints.

A photograph might make a room appear spacious, for example, while overlooking the ways in which railings, stairs or existing furniture restrict circulation.

By recording the dimensions directly, I could begin translating my visual understanding of the venue into something more useful for spatial planning.

From Measurements to a Blender Model

My next step is to reconstruct the venue in Blender.

Rather than producing a highly detailed architectural visualisation, I intend to create a relatively simple model that accurately represents the building's proportions and significant structural features.

The emphasis will be on spatial accuracy and usability rather than photorealism.

Walls, floors, staircases, railings, windows and existing furniture can all be represented using straightforward geometry and simple textures.

This approach should make the model considerably easier to navigate and maintain, while preserving the information necessary for planning.

I also intend to make the model accessible through a web browser using Three.js.

By exporting the Blender environment into a suitable format, I can create an interactive viewer that allows members of the organising team to inspect the venue without needing specialist 3D software.

I'm particularly interested in using back-face culling to help make the interior easier to explore, although the geometry and camera behaviour will need to be configured appropriately.

The aim is to create an accessible representation of the venue that prioritises clarity over visual complexity.

Developing an Interactive Planning Tool

The longer-term ambition is to make the environment more than a static 3D model.

Ideally, I'd like the organisers to be able to manipulate elements of the space directly.

For example, tables could be repositioned to test different configurations, helping us understand how different arrangements might affect circulation and the overall experience.

The same principle could eventually extend to equipment placement, cable routing, sightlines and other logistical considerations.

Having measured the positions of electrical sockets, I could incorporate those into the model to provide a useful reference when deciding where equipment might be installed.

This would transform the model into a practical planning tool rather than simply a visual representation.

I imagine it being particularly valuable during group discussions.

Instead of relying entirely on photographs, rough floor plans or verbal descriptions, everyone could refer to the same interactive environment and explore ideas collaboratively.

Of course, these interactive features are still ambitions rather than completed functionality.

For now, the immediate priority is to reconstruct the space accurately and establish a working online viewer.

Taking Initiative in a Collaborative Environment

One aspect of this project that I find particularly meaningful is the opportunity to contribute to something without necessarily having an assigned responsibility.

I wasn't asked to conduct a measured survey of the venue.

However, knowing that the organisers intended to hold a planning meeting, I recognised an opportunity to prepare something that could make that process easier.

I happened to have the day free while other members of the team were working, so it seemed like a sensible use of my time.

It's a relatively modest contribution, but one that could save considerable effort when the group begins exploring different arrangements.

I also think it reflects something we discussed months earlier: the value of bringing together people with different skills and giving them opportunities to contribute meaningfully.

For me, that is part of what makes creative communities so rewarding.

Not every contribution needs to be dramatic or immediately visible. Sometimes, simply preparing the groundwork allows other people to realise their ideas more effectively.

Looking Ahead

I'm excited to begin translating these measurements into a digital environment.

While the immediate purpose is to support the upcoming Darts of Death event, I'm also interested in the wider possibilities of accessible, browser-based 3D planning tools.

The combination of Blender and Three.js offers an opportunity to bring spatial design into a more collaborative and interactive environment.

Ultimately, I hope this small initiative will make the organising process smoother and give the team more freedom to experiment with the venue's layout.

Darts of Death is as much about bringing people together as it is about the tournament itself, and I'm looking forward to contributing to an event that celebrates creativity, collaboration and shared enjoyment.
