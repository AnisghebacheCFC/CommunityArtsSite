Finding the Rhythm: Editing, Collaboration and Creative Compromise

By Anis Ghebache

Tonight, I sent Liza the first rough cut of her film, covering three of the five scenes. With the deadline approaching, I'm increasingly conscious of how quickly time disappears during post-production. Having worked under tight deadlines before, I know that what initially feels like plenty of time can vanish surprisingly quickly.

This project will be taking priority alongside my ongoing work with Community Arts, and I've been gradually clearing my schedule to accommodate the more demanding stages ahead.

The First Rough Cut

The initial edit has been an opportunity to explore the film's visual language and establish its pacing.

One particular detail I noticed while reviewing the footage was how David frequently walks behind other characters, briefly disappearing from view. I found this visually compelling and began using these movements to transition between shots.

There's something quite beautiful about this recurring visual language. It creates a sense of continuity without drawing attention to the cuts themselves.

For example, in one sequence, David enters a room and appears almost immediately behind Catherine. I intentionally selected this arrangement because I liked its momentum and the way his movement connected the shots.

However, Liza preferred a different take.

Adopting her preferred shot meant removing approximately eight to ten seconds of footage and rearranging the surrounding sequence. The result was cleaner in certain respects, although it changed the pacing considerably.

There were also minor continuity challenges. Catherine's positioning differed between takes, and I had to carefully consider how David's movement carried across the cuts.

These discrepancies are often more apparent to an editor who has spent hours examining the footage than to someone watching the finished sequence.

It's an interesting balancing act between technical precision, visual continuity and emotional rhythm.

Interpreting Someone Else's Vision

One of the most interesting aspects of this process has been understanding the relationship between a director's intentions and an editor's interpretation.

My initial choices were informed by the footage itself: its movement, pacing and the visual patterns I discovered while assembling it.

Liza's preferences, meanwhile, are informed by something I couldn't have experienced directly: the thoughts and emotions she felt while directing and recording those moments.

Certain shots hold particular significance for her, and naturally she wants to preserve those feelings and communicate them to an audience.

I find that quite beautiful.

The film was shot approximately a year ago, and her vision has had time to develop and become more refined. She's been generous enough to consolidate her preferences into a PDF, including a visual breakdown of her preferred shots.

I'm grateful for that clarity, particularly because translating an idea from someone's imagination into an actual sequence is rarely straightforward.

What works beautifully in your head can feel entirely different once assembled on a timeline.

I've also found a certain relief in recognising that I don't need to impose my own interpretation on every moment.

Ultimately, I want Liza to recognise the finished film as hers.

My responsibility is to preserve her intentions while using my experience to maintain the pacing, continuity and atmosphere that will allow those intentions to reach the audience.

She's been commendably open to giving me creative freedom while remaining steadfast in her vision. Having experienced collaborations where that balance was difficult to achieve, I appreciate it enormously.

Rebuilding the Sound

Another challenge has been the audio.

The dialogue was recorded in a basement with brick walls and fabric surfaces, creating an awkward acoustic environment.

I used Premiere Pro's audio tools to isolate and clean the dialogue, with surprisingly effective results.

Perhaps a little too effective.

Liza pointed out that the dialogue sounded almost like a voiceover, which I completely understood. Removing unwanted environmental noise had also removed much of the acoustic character that made the voices feel situated within the scene.

The next stage will involve rebuilding that environment through sound design.

I'll be introducing subtle reverb, footsteps, boiler suit movements and other small environmental details. I'm also considering a low ambient hum to help bring the different elements together.

The intention isn't simply to make the audio sound realistic, but to make it feel as though the characters genuinely inhabit the same physical space.

Editing With VFX in Mind

One particularly interesting consideration has been how the editing decisions influence the visual effects workload.

I still need to remove unwanted elements from the footage and integrate the infinite back wall, which will require considerable masking, tracking and compositing.

Stationary wide shots are generally more manageable because the camera remains fixed. Moving handheld shots introduce additional complications, particularly when they reveal areas of the environment that will need extensive reconstruction.

I've therefore been deliberately arranging certain transitions to avoid unnecessarily complicated VFX work.

For example, cutting from a moving shot to a stationary wide shot can preserve the sequence while avoiding footage that would otherwise require significant cleanup.

But this introduces another artistic challenge.

Too many cuts, particularly between shaky handheld shots, can make the film feel unexpectedly casual. At times, the visual rhythm begins to resemble a mockumentary or sitcom, something closer to The Office than the hauntingly beautiful atmosphere Liza wants to create.

The challenge is finding a balance between the shots that best serve the film and the practical demands of completing it.

An efficient edit isn't necessarily an effective one.

Finding the Balance

Another consideration is communication.

Naturally, I want to keep Liza involved in decisions that influence her vision. However, continually requesting feedback on every adjustment risks slowing production and introducing unnecessary confusion.

Without immediate feedback, it can be tempting to seek reassurance that each decision is correct.

But editing also requires trust.

Having established a clearer understanding of her intentions, I need to exercise my own judgement and make decisions that support the film as a whole.

The rough cut has been particularly valuable in this respect. It's given us something tangible to respond to, allowing ideas that previously existed as notes, memories and intentions to be tested against the actual footage.

There is still considerable work ahead, particularly with the VFX, sound design and remaining scenes.

But I'm looking forward to it.

What I'm finding most rewarding is the opportunity to reconcile different perspectives without diminishing either of them.

The director brings an emotional relationship with the material that is uniquely hers. The editor brings another perspective, informed by rhythm, continuity and the possibilities revealed through the footage.

The goal isn't for one interpretation to overpower the other.

It's to find the version of the film in which those perspectives work together, and hopefully create something neither of us could have achieved alone.
