Featured Artwork and the Problem of Collective Growth

By Anis Ghebache

Before I began developing the tower, I'd considered using the website to display a changing selection of artwork.

The original idea was relatively straightforward: each month, an artwork could be featured within the environment, perhaps replacing the backdrop behind an animated character.

Although the website has since evolved considerably, I haven't abandoned that idea, and I'm now exploring how to incorporate featured artwork into the tower itself.

Making the Artwork Part of the Environment

One possibility I've been considering is suspending a framed artwork from the brick structure.

Rather than displaying the image in a conventional gallery interface, I want it to exist as an object within the three-dimensional world.

I particularly like the idea of the frame hanging from a bracket and responding to the tower's movement.

As visitors rotate the structure, the frame could swing with the momentum, lagging slightly behind the rotation before gradually settling!

This could be achieved through a relatively lightweight animation system using spring-like movement and damping, without requiring an elaborate physics simulation.

It's a small interaction, but one that could make the environment feel more alive.

Designing Around Growth

The difficulty is that the tower doesn't begin as a completed structure. Its height depends on the number of bricks contributed by visitors. In the current prototype, each complete layer contains 30 bricks.

Attaching a framed artwork to the tower would therefore require enough bricks to support the visual arrangement.

If the frame needs several layers of brickwork to look appropriate, it might be some time before the structure reaches that point.

This creates an interesting design problem.

I want the artwork to feel physically connected to the tower, but I don't want its visibility to depend on how quickly people contribute.

Visibility From the Beginning

For me, displaying participants' work from the first day is important.

Community Arts exists to provide opportunities for people to make and share artwork. If I'm encouraging people to participate creatively, I want that work to receive attention from the outset.

The featured artwork would primarily come from our Community Arts sessions, although I may occasionally include other pieces.

I also want to retain editorial control over what appears, rather than establish a rigid monthly schedule before understanding how the feature works in practice.

The implementation is still unresolved.

One possibility is a separate support that accommodates the artwork while the tower is small, with the frame eventually becoming attached to the structure as it grows.

However, I haven't settled on an arrangement, and I'd rather test how the different possibilities look before making that decision.

Giving the Work a Presence

What interests me most is the possibility of making the featured artwork something visitors encounter within the environment, rather than another image placed on a webpage.

The frame's movement, its relationship to the tower, and its changing position could all contribute to that experience.

At the same time, I don't want it to obscure the individual bricks or interfere with people's ability to find their contributions.

I'm beginning to appreciate how an environment designed around collective participation introduces considerations that wouldn't exist in a conventional static composition.

It's not simply a question of where I want to place an artwork. It's a question of how to accommodate that artwork within a structure whose development is determined by other people.

The final solution is something I still need to explore, but the intention is clear: the artwork should have a visible, valued place in the environment from the very beginning.
