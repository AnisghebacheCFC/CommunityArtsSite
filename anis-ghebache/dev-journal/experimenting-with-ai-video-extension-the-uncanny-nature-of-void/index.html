Experimenting with AI Video Extension: The Uncanny Nature of Void

By Anis Ghebache

Following my initial meeting with Liza, I began experimenting with Adobe Premiere Pro's generative AI tools to explore how they might help address some of the pacing challenges in her short film, Void.

The experiment focused on a particular shot in which David has just entered the scene and looks towards Charlotte before asking her about the chair. It's a wonderfully expressive over-the-shoulder shot, and I felt that extending his moment of hesitation could create additional tension.

The challenge was that the footage simply didn't contain the extra seconds needed to achieve this through conventional editing.

Generating the Missing Moment

I used Premiere Pro's Generative Extend functionality, experimenting with reference frames to produce approximately five seconds of additional footage.

The original footage was particularly challenging. It had a distinctive grainy quality, compounded by what appeared to be a digital zoom. As the camera moved closer to David, the grain became increasingly pronounced, alongside the movement of the handheld camera.

The generated footage, however, looked noticeably different. It was more saturated, sharper and more defined than the original, which had a softness beneath its grain.

Interestingly, although the original footage was recorded in 4K and the generated footage was 1080p, the AI appeared to reconstruct and refine details that weren't clearly visible in the source material.

This was particularly noticeable in David's face, where subtle changes in the shadows altered the appearance of his facial structure.

Trying to Make the Footage Seamless

Much of my time was spent attempting to reconcile these differences.

I initially experimented with converting the original footage's colour characteristics into a LUT and applying it to the generated material, with varying degrees of success.

I subsequently moved towards manual colour grading, adjusting the footage more selectively. One particularly frustrating challenge was the inconsistency of the AI's colour interpretation. David's hair and skin, for example, required different corrections.

I also experimented with Morph Cut to soften the transition between the original and generated footage.

Another challenge was reproducing the handheld camera movement. The original shot contained subtle changes in pitch and yaw, amplified by the digital zoom. I experimented with Premiere Pro's camera shake effects to recreate this movement and make the extension feel more consistent with the original cinematography.

The result was reasonably convincing at a glance, although something about it remained unsettling.

The Uncanny Result

What interested me most was how close the generated footage came to appearing authentic without quite achieving it.

David's facial features changed subtly throughout the extension. Even after correcting the colour and attempting to reproduce the camera movement, there was a perceptual inconsistency that made the footage feel uncanny.

Everything looked almost right, but something was noticeably wrong.

Given Void's hauntingly beautiful atmosphere and underlying existential absurdism, this wasn't necessarily an undesirable quality. An unsettling visual effect could potentially contribute to the film's atmosphere.

However, that wasn't the objective of this particular experiment.

I wanted to extend a pause in the performance and improve the pacing. After reviewing the result, I realised that the additional footage didn't meaningfully enhance the scene.

Understanding the Limitations

One of the biggest lessons from this experiment was the difficulty generative video tools have with preserving the characteristics of heavily grained footage.

Grain introduces considerable complexity, particularly when it obscures fine details such as facial features. Generative models can reconstruct plausible information, but that doesn't necessarily mean the reconstructed details will remain faithful to the original.

There are potential workarounds I'd like to explore.

One possibility would be to remove the grain before extending the footage, then reintroduce it afterwards. Another would involve experimenting with style transfer or other methods of matching the visual characteristics of the original material.

However, neither approach necessarily resolves inconsistencies in facial structure or performance.

I'm conscious that these technologies are developing rapidly, and what proves difficult today may become considerably more achievable in the future.

Working With the Tools, Not Against Them

Ultimately, I've decided that this particular extension probably won't make it into the final edit of Void.

Nevertheless, the experiment was valuable. It gave me an opportunity to explore Premiere Pro's emerging generative capabilities while confronting some of their practical limitations.

I also came away with a clearer understanding of where these tools might be most effective.

Close-ups of human faces are particularly unforgiving because even subtle inconsistencies can be immediately recognisable. Other compositions may offer considerably more freedom for experimentation without drawing attention to the artificial nature of the footage.

For me, filmmaking has always involved understanding the strengths and limitations of the tools available.

Part of the magic lies in knowing where to direct the audience's attention, what to reveal and what to conceal.

Rather than forcing a technique into a scene simply because it's possible, I want to use these experiments to develop a more considered understanding of when emerging technologies can genuinely contribute to storytelling.

And sometimes, the most useful outcome of an experiment is discovering that the original footage works better without it.
