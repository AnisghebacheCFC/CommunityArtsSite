Designing for Uncertainty: Reflections on Teaching Unreal Engine

By Anis Ghebache

From Lesson Planning to Practical Delivery

Recently, I developed and delivered a practical teaching demonstration using Unreal Engine, exploring how technical concepts can be communicated effectively to students studying Games Art.

My preparation involved developing a structured lesson plan, rehearsing technical demonstrations and considering how students with different levels of software experience might engage with the material.

Drawing on my background in 3D animation and my experience as a VFX Specialist Technician at the University of the Arts London, I wanted to approach the session from both a technical and pedagogical perspective.

The objective was not simply to demonstrate functionality within Unreal Engine, but to consider how existing knowledge could be transferred between different creative applications, making unfamiliar software more accessible.

Although I had carefully rehearsed the session, its delivery introduced several unexpected challenges. These provided an opportunity to examine the relationship between structured preparation, technical adaptability and effective communication.

Preparing for Different Levels of Technical Experience

One consideration during the planning process was the existing software knowledge of Games Art students.

Many creative applications share underlying principles, even when their interfaces and workflows differ considerably. Understanding these similarities can help educators introduce unfamiliar software without requiring students to approach every concept from the beginning.

For example, students familiar with Blender may already understand fundamental principles of three-dimensional navigation, object manipulation and spatial organisation.

However, the navigation conventions used in Blender differ from those found in Unreal Engine.

I therefore planned to demonstrate how Unreal Engine's navigation preferences could be adjusted to accommodate workflows that students might already recognise.

This was intended to illustrate a broader teaching principle: new technical knowledge can often be introduced more effectively by connecting it to existing competencies.

Rather than treating software applications as entirely separate disciplines, I wanted to encourage an understanding of the transferable concepts that underpin digital creative practice.

When the Technical Environment Differs from the Lesson Plan

One of the most significant challenges emerged when the available Unreal Engine environment differed from the one I had used during preparation.

Although the project contained the same general asset pack, its level layout was substantially different.

Several elements I had intended to incorporate into my demonstration, including dynamic blocks and larger geometric objects, were absent.

This presented an immediate challenge because parts of my lesson had been structured around interacting with those particular assets.

Additionally, the installed version of Unreal Engine differed from my own, introducing changes to the interface and the location of certain settings.

During the navigation demonstration, I attempted to locate a setting that was readily accessible in the version I had rehearsed. However, the interface had changed, and the option was not where I expected it to be.

Rather than allowing the search for a particular setting to interrupt the wider session, I acknowledged the version difference and continued.

This experience reinforced the importance of distinguishing between the essential learning objective and the specific technical procedure used to demonstrate it.

A lesson should ideally remain educationally valuable even when the exact sequence of software interactions cannot be reproduced.

For creative technology educators, this is particularly relevant. Software updates, interface changes and differences in institutional hardware configurations are common features of the teaching environment.

Consequently, adaptability is not simply a useful supplementary skill. It is an important component of effective technical instruction.

Contingency Planning and Software Compatibility

Anticipating the possibility of technical difficulties, I had prepared an additional Unreal Engine project file that could be used as an alternative starting point.

The intention was to provide a reliable fallback if the supplied environment proved unsuitable for the planned demonstration.

However, the backup project was incompatible with the installed version of Unreal Engine.

This exposed an important limitation in my contingency planning.

Although preparing alternative resources is valuable, those resources must also be suitable for the environment in which they are intended to operate.

In retrospect, a more comprehensive approach would involve preparing several layers of contingency.

For example, a practical teaching session could include a primary project, an alternative demonstration using standard software features and a conceptual explanation that remains accessible even if the intended project files cannot be opened.

This approach would help ensure that the educational objectives remain achievable independently of particular technical dependencies.

It also raises a broader question about how technical lessons should be designed.

Should teaching demonstrations depend on precisely configured environments, or should they be structured to accommodate a reasonable degree of technical uncertainty?

I increasingly favour the latter, particularly when working with software that evolves rapidly.

The Relationship Between Technical Knowledge and Teaching Fluency

Another important aspect of the experience was evaluating the fluency of my delivery.

As someone who regularly works with creative software, I am accustomed to navigating technical environments, troubleshooting problems and moving between different applications.

However, demonstrating a process while simultaneously explaining it introduces an additional layer of complexity.

Technical instruction requires the educator to consider not only the actions being performed, but also the clarity, pacing and relevance of the accompanying explanation.

During the session, I recognised moments where my delivery could have been more fluid.

Encountering unfamiliar interface elements occasionally interrupted the rhythm of the demonstration, highlighting the distinction between understanding a technical process and communicating it effectively in real time.

This is an area I intend to develop through more deliberate rehearsal.

Rather than focusing exclusively on completing a sequence of technical actions, future preparation will also involve practising the verbal explanations, transitions and alternative approaches that accompany them.

I believe this distinction is particularly important when teaching practical creative subjects.

Students benefit not only from observing successful technical procedures, but also from understanding the reasoning behind each action and how those principles can be applied independently.

Communicating Technical Knowledge with Precision

My broader experience encompasses several creative applications, including Unreal Engine, Blender, Houdini, Nuke and After Effects.

Working across these applications has encouraged me to recognise similarities and differences between workflows, particularly in areas such as procedural systems, compositing and digital content creation.

However, breadth of technical knowledge introduces its own communication challenges.

It can be tempting to expand an explanation into related subjects, particularly when discussing familiar tools or processes.

Reflecting on my delivery and subsequent discussions, I recognised the importance of selecting information according to its immediate relevance.

An extensive explanation is not necessarily a more effective one.

In educational settings, introducing too many adjacent concepts can obscure the central learning objective, particularly for students who are still developing their understanding of the subject.

Similarly, in professional discussions, providing excessive technical context can make an otherwise straightforward answer more difficult to follow.

This has encouraged me to think more deliberately about the relationship between technical depth and communication.

The goal is not to simplify complex subjects unnecessarily, but to structure information so that its relevance remains clear.

An effective educator must therefore exercise judgement over what to explain, when to introduce additional detail and when to allow a concept to stand on its own.

Developing a More Adaptable Teaching Methodology

Reflecting on the experience, I have identified several principles that I intend to incorporate into future lesson planning.

Prioritising learning objectives over specific procedures

A demonstration should be structured around what students are expected to understand or accomplish, rather than depending entirely on a predetermined sequence of interactions.

This allows alternative methods to be introduced without compromising the central purpose of the lesson.

Designing technical contingencies

Where possible, I intend to verify software versions, project compatibility and required assets before practical sessions.

When these cannot be confirmed, alternative demonstrations and explanations should be prepared to accommodate unexpected differences.

Rehearsing communication alongside technical execution

Technical familiarity alone does not guarantee fluent instruction.

I intend to practise the explanations and transitions accompanying each demonstration, allowing greater attention to be directed towards the students and their understanding during delivery.

Building upon existing student knowledge

Introducing unfamiliar software through concepts students already understand can make technical education more accessible.

This approach also encourages students to recognise transferable skills rather than viewing each application as an isolated discipline.

Maintaining clarity and relevance

I want to become more deliberate in selecting the information I communicate, ensuring that additional technical detail supports rather than distracts from the intended learning outcome.

Resilience and Professional Development

Beyond the technical challenges, the experience also reinforced the importance of remaining composed when circumstances differ from expectations.

Unexpected logistical difficulties had significantly reduced the preparation time I had originally allocated before the session.

Although I had rehearsed my material beforehand, I was unable to complete the final review and familiarisation period I had planned.

Combined with the differences in the technical environment, this required me to adjust my expectations and respond to the circumstances as they unfolded.

One lesson I have taken from this is that preparation should not depend entirely on favourable conditions.

While it remains important to plan carefully, professional competence also involves making informed decisions when those plans cannot be followed exactly.

I do not consider adaptability a substitute for preparation. Rather, I see it as something that should be developed alongside it.

The more thoroughly I understand the underlying principles of a subject, the more effectively I can adapt how those principles are communicated.

Closing Reflections

This experience provided an opportunity to examine several aspects of my developing teaching practice, from technical preparation and software compatibility to instructional fluency and communication.

It also encouraged me to consider how lesson planning can become more resilient without sacrificing structure or educational depth.

As I continue developing my work across creative technology and education, I am increasingly interested in the relationship between technical expertise and effective pedagogy.

Knowing how to use a piece of software is only one part of teaching it.

The greater challenge is understanding how to communicate that knowledge in a way that is accessible, relevant and adaptable to the needs of different learners.

Ultimately, I want my teaching practice to be characterised not only by technical proficiency, but also by thoughtful preparation, clear communication and the ability to respond constructively to uncertainty.

These are qualities that I intend to continue developing through practical experience, reflection and continued engagement with creative education.
