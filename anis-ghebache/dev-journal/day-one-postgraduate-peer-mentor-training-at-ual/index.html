Day One: Postgraduate Peer Mentor Training at UAL

By Anis Ghebache

On Wednesday 21 October, I attended my first training session for the University of the Arts London's Postgraduate Peer Mentorship Programme at London College of Communication.

The session was scheduled from 10 AM to 3 PM in room T1007, although I arrived later because of my existing commitment to facilitating Community Arts workshops at Draper Hall.

Balancing these responsibilities has become an increasingly familiar part of my routine. Alongside developing my own artistic practice, I've been trying to establish opportunities that allow me to contribute meaningfully to the creative development of others.

The mentorship programme seemed like a natural extension of this work, offering an opportunity to support students who are beginning to navigate an environment I have already experienced.

Understanding the Responsibilities of a Mentor

Much of the training explored the responsibilities and boundaries associated with being a peer mentor.

One particularly interesting exercise involved discussing hypothetical scenarios in which mentors might find themselves navigating ambiguous social situations.

For example, we considered what might happen if a mentee offered their mentor a gift.

Initially, this might seem like a straightforward question of whether accepting gifts is appropriate. However, the discussion introduced several additional considerations.

There is an important distinction between a small, thoughtful gesture of appreciation and something of considerable financial value.

Cultural expectations can also influence how gifts are understood. In some circumstances, refusing an offering might be interpreted as impolite, even when the intention behind that refusal is to maintain professional boundaries.

I found this particularly interesting because it illustrated how social situations rarely fit neatly into universal rules.

A mentor needs to exercise judgement, taking into account both the intentions of the individual and the responsibilities associated with their position.

We also discussed situations involving social invitations, including the possibility of being invited to parties.

These scenarios raised similar questions about maintaining appropriate boundaries without becoming unnecessarily distant or impersonal.

Mentorship as a Relationship

One of the ideas I took away from the session was that mentorship involves finding a balance between approachability and responsibility.

A peer mentor is not simply an authority figure dispensing instructions. The relationship should allow students to feel comfortable discussing their experiences, uncertainties and ambitions.

At the same time, the mentor needs to recognise the limits of their role.

I've encountered similar questions through my work with Community Arts, particularly when supporting people with different levels of creative experience.

I increasingly believe that creating an environment where people feel comfortable participating is often more valuable than immediately attempting to direct their decisions.

Being supportive doesn't necessarily mean providing an answer to every problem.

Sometimes it means listening carefully, asking useful questions and helping someone develop the confidence to reach their own conclusions.

Introducing the Programme to Animation Students

Following the training, I decided to visit some of the animation students at LCC.

I first went to see the graduating cohort, whom I already knew through my involvement with the course.

I then visited a classroom nearby where the new cohort was studying.

After obtaining permission from the lecturer, I briefly introduced myself and explained the peer mentorship programme.

I wanted students to know that the opportunity existed and that they could engage with someone who had already experienced many of the challenges associated with studying animation at postgraduate level.

The announcement lasted approximately five minutes.

It was a relatively small intervention, but I felt that speaking directly to students was more meaningful than relying entirely on an email or institutional announcement.

Later, Louise, who was coordinating the programme, mentioned that approximately three students from the classroom had registered their interest.

Although I couldn't be certain whether they would ultimately be assigned to me, I was pleased that the introduction had encouraged some engagement.

Finding Structure in Spontaneity

Reflecting on why I wanted to become a peer mentor, I realised that helping others has always been something I've enjoyed.

However, my efforts have often been sporadic.

I'm naturally spontaneous, and much of my engagement with other people has emerged instinctively, through unexpected conversations, chance encounters or opportunities that happen to present themselves.

Although I value this aspect of my personality, I've become increasingly interested in how I might give these impulses a more consistent structure.

The mentorship programme offers exactly that possibility.

Rather than waiting for opportunities to help somebody to arise by chance, I can establish a continuing relationship in which support, encouragement and the exchange of ideas become intentional.

I don't necessarily want to change the spontaneous qualities of my personality. In many ways, I believe they're part of what allows me to connect with people.

Instead, I'm interested in creating circumstances in which those qualities can become useful to others over a longer period.

If my natural disposition, experiences and individual character traits can help someone discover an opportunity, develop their confidence or find greater joy and purpose in what they're doing, then I'd be genuinely happy to make that part of my own purpose.

There's something particularly rewarding about the possibility that a relatively ordinary interaction might have a lasting positive influence on another person's life.

I don't expect to transform someone's experience through a single conversation, nor do I believe mentorship needs to operate through grand gestures.

Sometimes simply being available, offering another perspective or providing someone with the opportunity to talk through their ideas can be meaningful.

What appeals to me is the possibility of making those interactions more consistent.

An Unexpected Reflection

Around the time I received my peer mentorship contract, I came across a small leaflet resting on a public bench.

It read: "Have you thought about your soul?"

I believe it was a leaflet promoting a local church.

At the time, the message didn't hold any particular significance for me.

Nevertheless, something about the scene caught my attention, and I decided to photograph it.

Now, reflecting on the experience of joining the mentorship programme, I find it interesting that I took the photograph at all.

What was initially an ordinary, almost inconsequential observation has acquired a different meaning through the circumstances that followed.

I've been thinking more seriously about the kind of person I want to become, the opportunities I want to create and the impact I might have on those around me.

Although I wouldn't necessarily interpret the leaflet's question in a religious sense, it now feels unexpectedly relevant to those considerations.

Perhaps there's something interesting about how meaning can emerge retrospectively.

An image or experience that seems insignificant in the moment can become a useful point of reflection when revisited from a different perspective.

I find it amusing that such a simple photograph has found its way into a journal entry about mentorship, but perhaps that's precisely why I wanted to include it.

Looking Ahead

My first day introduced me to some of the interpersonal responsibilities involved in mentorship, particularly the importance of maintaining thoughtful boundaries while remaining approachable.

It also gave me an opportunity to begin connecting the programme with students who might benefit from it.

I'm interested in seeing how these relationships develop and whether my experiences in animation, creative technology and community facilitation can help others navigate their own creative journeys.

More broadly, I see mentorship as another opportunity to develop the interpersonal side of my artistic practice.

I've spent considerable time developing my technical and creative abilities, but I'm increasingly interested in what those abilities might enable me to contribute beyond my own work.

If I can use my experiences and natural inclination towards helping others to create positive change, however modest, then that's something I'd like to pursue deliberately.

For me, the programme represents an opportunity to turn something that has historically been spontaneous into a more sustained commitment.

And perhaps, in doing so, discover something about my own sense of purpose along the way.
