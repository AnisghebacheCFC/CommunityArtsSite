How Long Should a Shot Hold? Editing Void

By Anis Ghebache

I sent Liza a first rough cut of three of the five scenes in Void. One of the things I kept coming back to while editing was how often David walks behind another character and briefly disappears. I started cutting on those movements. For a moment your eye is still following him, and by the time he comes back into view, you’re already in the next shot. I liked how quietly that worked.

In one sequence, David enters the room and appears almost immediately behind Catherine. I liked the momentum of that arrangement, but Liza preferred a different take. Working it in took roughly eight to ten seconds out of the sequence. I then had to find a way through the surrounding shots because Catherine was standing in a slightly different position. I’d been looking at her position for so long that the mismatch felt enormous. Watching the sequence back, I was more interested in what those missing seconds did to the rhythm.

When the camera changes the tone

Some of the wide shots are locked off, which makes them easier to work with when it comes to replacing the brick wall with a space that feels as though it goes on indefinitely. The handheld shots can reveal more of the room as the camera moves, so keeping one in the edit can mean reconstructing a much larger part of the background.

I thought I could manage some of that work by cutting away from the handheld shots sooner. The funny thing was that, once I started moving between angles more frequently, the scene began to feel a bit like The Office. The handheld movement and the quicker cuts gave it a casual, almost comic rhythm. Liza wants the film to feel hauntingly beautiful, and I could hear how far I’d moved from that in the rhythm of the edit alone. It made me pay more attention to the shots that gave me time to look around the room and sit with how strange it felt.

Giving the voices their room back

I had a similar experience with the dialogue. Premiere cleaned up the basement recording so effectively that Liza said the voices sounded like voiceover. Once she pointed it out, I could hear it immediately. The voices were clear, but they felt strangely close, even in a wide shot.

I want to bring back some of the room through footsteps, the rustle of the boiler suits and a little reverb. I’m also considering a low hum. I’m curious how little it will take for the characters to feel as though they’re standing in the same room again, especially in the pauses between lines.

Making decisions between cuts

Liza put together a PDF of the shots she wanted to use, which has helped me understand what she remembers and values about the performances. I’ve spent hours with the footage, and she spent time with the actors making it. Sometimes she’ll point me towards a take I’d passed over, and I’ll see a gesture or a look I missed because I was thinking about how to get into the next shot.

I’ve also realised that asking her about every adjustment can make it harder for either of us to judge the scene. A decision that sounds significant in a message might pass almost imperceptibly in the cut. I’m getting more comfortable making those smaller calls and then watching the whole sequence back. I want to see where my attention goes, when I start to feel uneasy, and whether I’ve given that feeling enough time to settle before I cut away.
