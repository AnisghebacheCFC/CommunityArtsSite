From Burlap Friends to Handmade Clothing: Exploring Bleach Illustration

By Anis Ghebache

Developing the Idea

I've always enjoyed 2D drawing and animation, particularly the expressive possibilities of simple character designs.

While developing my animated short film Burlap Friends, I established a visual language built around relatively uncomplicated characters whose personalities could be communicated through exaggerated expressions, gestures and movement.

I wanted to explore how this aesthetic might extend beyond animation into something physical.

During a conversation with Anna, my fellow Community Arts workshop facilitator, I proposed the possibility of developing a small clothing brand featuring original illustrations.

The idea initially emerged in connection with the UAL Arts Market, which seemed like an interesting opportunity to experiment with selling handmade creative work.

Unfortunately, we didn't manage to produce anything before the 4 November deadline. Nevertheless, the concept remained something I was keen to pursue independently.

Rather than treating the missed deadline as the end of the project, I began considering the creative and practical possibilities of producing illustrated clothing ourselves.

Drawing with Bleach

My initial idea was to create illustrations directly onto black cotton T-shirts using bleach.

I was particularly interested in how this technique could reproduce the clean, expressive linework associated with Burlap Friends while introducing an element of unpredictability.

Unlike a conventional printed design, applying bleach to fabric would involve physically altering the colour of the material itself.

The resulting lines could vary depending on the fabric, concentration, application and duration of exposure.

I found this particularly appealing because it would allow each garment to retain subtle irregularities, making the handmade nature of the work immediately apparent.

My intention wasn't to reproduce a digital illustration with absolute precision.

Instead, I wanted to preserve the recognisable qualities of the original designs while allowing the material and production process to contribute their own visual character.

The Value of Imperfection

One of the principles I've become increasingly interested in through Community Arts is the importance of celebrating human creativity.

Working with participants during our workshops has reinforced my appreciation for the qualities that emerge when people engage directly with materials.

Marks don't always behave exactly as intended. Colours interact unpredictably, lines vary in thickness and individual gestures leave traces of the person responsible for making them.

Rather than interpreting these qualities as imperfections that need correcting, I often find that they contribute to the character of the finished work.

I wanted to explore a similar philosophy through clothing.

The simplicity of the Burlap Friends characters seemed particularly well suited to this approach.

Their designs rely on relatively few lines, allowing their expressions and silhouettes to remain recognisable without extensive detail.

At the same time, their playful visual identity could provide an interesting contrast to the rougher, handmade qualities of bleach illustration.

Developing a Visual Identity

Although the initial idea involved transferring existing characters onto garments, I began considering the potential for creating entirely new illustrations specifically designed for clothing.

This raised several interesting questions about composition.

An image created for a film, poster or digital screen doesn't necessarily function equally well when transferred onto fabric.

The size and placement of the illustration, the shape of the garment and the distance from which someone views it all influence how the design is perceived.

I was interested in developing illustrations that retained the humour, expressiveness and slightly unsettling qualities of Burlap Friends, while also functioning as distinctive pieces of graphic design.

The clothing itself could become another way of extending the visual identity I'd developed through animation.

Looking Ahead

At this stage, the project remains an exploration rather than an established clothing brand.

The underlying production concept is straightforward, but I still need to conduct physical experiments to understand how successfully the illustrations translate onto fabric.

I already have equipment available for experimenting with the technique, although finding time for production has been difficult alongside my other responsibilities.

Nevertheless, I'm interested in pursuing the idea because it brings together several aspects of my creative practice: illustration, animation, material experimentation and an appreciation for handmade work.

I particularly like the possibility of producing garments that are visually consistent with one another while remaining individually distinctive through the imperfections introduced during their creation.

For now, my next step is to develop illustrations that could eventually become suitable designs for the garments.
