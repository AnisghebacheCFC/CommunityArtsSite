AI for Film and TV: Authorship, Ownership and Creative Control

By Anis Ghebache

An Introduction to the Legal Landscape

Today's online session of the National Film and Television School's AI Certificate explored the legal, ethical and commercial implications of generative AI within the creative industries.

The discussion was led by Cody Updegrave of Ambiguous and Jack Jones, a partner at Sheridans specialising in interactive creative media and emerging technologies.

Much of the material was familiar to me, having followed developments in artificial intelligence relatively closely. Nevertheless, the session offered a useful opportunity to consider how these technologies are being incorporated into professional production environments, particularly where intellectual property, performers' rights and commercial licensing are concerned.

Several examples illustrated how rapidly the industry is adapting. These included synthetic voice performances in video games, AI-generated fashion imagery, digital doubles and the licensing of existing creative material for model training.

What interested me most was the relationship between artistic authorship and legal ownership. As generative technologies become increasingly capable, the distinction between creating something, controlling its creation and possessing the legal rights to it becomes more complicated.

Authorship Beyond the Prompt

One particularly interesting aspect of the discussion concerned the different approaches to copyright protection across jurisdictions.

In the United States, copyright protection generally requires sufficient human authorship. Merely entering prompts into a generative system does not ordinarily establish copyright over its output. However, human modifications, arrangements and other original contributions may qualify for protection.

Chinese courts have, in certain cases, recognised copyright in AI-assisted imagery where sufficient human creative contribution could be demonstrated.

These differences raise an important question: at what point does the use of a generative tool constitute artistic authorship?

My own position is relatively straightforward. When working with generative AI, I have never felt that I was delegating creativity to another entity. I consider myself the creator.

Even when a model produces something unexpected, I remain responsible for evaluating the result, identifying its potential and determining whether it contributes to my intended outcome. The process involves continuous selection, rejection, adjustment and refinement.

Unpredictability is not unique to artificial intelligence. Traditional creative processes frequently produce unintended results, some of which become essential to the finished work.

I therefore find it difficult to accept that unpredictability alone diminishes authorship. What matters to me is the creative intention guiding the process and the decisions through which the final work takes shape.

Of course, my understanding of authorship does not necessarily correspond to its legal definition. That distinction is worth investigating further.

Democratising Creative Production

I see generative AI as a significant democratising force within filmmaking.

Historically, realising an ambitious creative vision has required access to substantial resources, specialist equipment and teams of practitioners working across different disciplines.

While collaboration can enrich a project, it also introduces additional layers of interpretation. An idea may change considerably as it moves between departments, each contributing its own understanding of the intended outcome.

Generative AI potentially reduces the number of intermediaries required to translate an idea into a finished piece.

For me, this represents more than an improvement in production efficiency. It offers the possibility of preserving artistic intention with greater consistency.

An individual artist can increasingly influence cinematography, production design, animation and other aspects of production without necessarily depending on separate specialists to execute every decision.

This does not mean collaboration becomes undesirable or that technical expertise loses its value. My background in 3D animation continues to inform how I evaluate images, movement, composition and visual coherence.

Rather, I believe the relationship between specialisation and creative direction is changing.

The artist may increasingly operate across disciplines, drawing upon technical understanding while concentrating more heavily on judgment, intention and the overall coherence of a project.

The Changing Value of Creative Labour

The democratisation of production also introduces difficult questions about employment and professional opportunity.

One example discussed during the session involved licensing a recognisable voice for use in a video game, allowing synthetic commentary to be generated within an agreed commercial framework.

I can appreciate the opportunities this creates for established performers, who may be able to license their likeness or voice for additional income.

However, I am less optimistic about the implications for those attempting to enter the industry.

If synthetic voices can fulfil an increasing proportion of routine commercial requirements, emerging voice actors may find themselves competing against inexpensive generated alternatives and licensed replicas of established performers.

This raises a concern that extends beyond immediate job displacement. The industry could begin reducing the very opportunities through which future talent would ordinarily develop experience and establish a professional reputation.

There is an uncomfortable contradiction here. The same technology that makes independent creative production more accessible may simultaneously make certain creative careers less accessible.

I do not think this contradiction invalidates the technology's potential, but it deserves consideration.

Who Determines the Rules?

Another recurring theme was the influence of producers, commissioners, lawyers and commercial rights holders in establishing the conditions under which AI can be used.

In the absence of comprehensive legal certainty, contractual agreements are becoming an important mechanism for determining acceptable practices.

I would prefer artists to have a meaningful influence over these standards, although historically, creative practitioners have not always possessed the greatest negotiating power within their industries.

Trade unions and collective bargaining can help address this imbalance. In the United States, for example, performers' agreements have introduced protections concerning consent and compensation for certain uses of AI-generated digital replicas.

Such protections can create additional production requirements, but they also raise a legitimate question about how technological innovation should be balanced against the interests of working artists.

I am particularly interested in whether future governance will encourage independent experimentation or disproportionately favour organisations with the legal and financial resources to navigate increasingly complicated licensing arrangements.

If generative AI has the potential to democratise production, its regulatory and commercial structures should ideally avoid undermining that accessibility.

Creative Intention as a Constant

Perhaps my strongest conviction following the session concerns the relationship between technology and artistic intention.

Generative AI has made me feel more creatively empowered, largely because it reduces the time required to explore and develop ideas.

The iterative process becomes considerably more fluid when I can generate variations, evaluate possibilities and make adjustments without repeatedly encountering the technical or logistical barriers associated with conventional production.

I find this especially valuable because it allows me to maintain momentum.

Rather than treating each technical obstacle as a separate production challenge, I can concentrate on the relationship between the work I am producing and the idea I am attempting to communicate.

I do not believe that using generative AI makes the resulting work inherently less personal.

If anything, having greater control over multiple aspects of production can make the final outcome more closely representative of an individual artistic vision.

The challenge is not simply learning how to generate convincing material. It is developing the discernment to recognise what is worth producing, why it matters and how each decision contributes to a coherent whole.

Looking Ahead

I see this course as a framework through which to develop my existing practice rather than as an introduction to artificial intelligence itself.

My intention is to use the coming months to experiment extensively, establish a substantial body of work and develop a portfolio that reflects my interests in animation, filmmaking and art direction.

The NFTS accreditation will provide additional professional recognition, but I consider the work produced throughout the process to be the more meaningful measure of progress.

I am also looking forward to learning from practitioners whose experience spans different areas of the creative industries.

Although this introductory session focused primarily on the legal and commercial environment, it provided a useful foundation for thinking about the wider implications of the tools I intend to explore.

I expect my opinions to develop as I encounter new technologies, production methods and perspectives.

For now, however, my position remains clear: generative AI does not necessarily distance the artist from the creative process. Used deliberately, it can bring the artist closer to the realisation of their original intention.

That is the possibility I am most interested in exploring.
