Strength Retention and Changing Training Demands

By Anis Ghebache

Returning to Heavy Training

After taking a relatively light day on Monday, I returned to the gym for my chest and triceps session.

I've recently noticed a reduction in my body weight, amounting to several kilograms over approximately a week.

This has coincided with an increase in my overall training volume, particularly since introducing smaller resistance exercises throughout the day.

I've also increased my protein intake, although my carbohydrate consumption has been somewhat lower than usual.

I suspect the combination of increased energy expenditure and changes in my diet is contributing to the weight loss, although it's difficult to determine how much represents fat rather than changes in glycogen, water or other fluctuations.

Given these changes, I was particularly interested in seeing how my strength would hold up.

Bench Press and Strength Retention

I began with the flat dumbbell bench press, which is generally the first exercise in my chest routine.

I was pleased to reach my usual maximum, particularly because I'd recently spent approximately two weeks recovering from an illness that had significantly reduced both my training volume and appetite.

Despite the interruption, I appeared to have retained almost all of my previous strength.

My subsequent sets were somewhat weaker, although I wasn't particularly concerned by this.

I've been increasing my overall training demands while simultaneously losing weight, so some variation in performance isn't entirely unexpected.

For now, I'm prioritising relatively heavy weights and lower repetitions, with the intention of preserving as much muscular strength and mass as possible.

Of course, lifting heavy is only one component of maintaining muscle during weight loss. Adequate nutrition and recovery remain important, particularly when overall training volume is increasing.

Adjusting My Overhead Press

My usual routine involves progressing from flat bench press to incline bench press, followed by overhead pressing and dips.

During the overhead press, however, I noticed some residual discomfort in my lower back.

I realised that the position I was adopting involved a degree of spinal extension, which appeared to place additional strain on the area affected by Sunday's furniture moving.

To address this, I adjusted the bench to a slightly reclined position, allowing me to lean back and receive more support.

This made the movement more comfortable, although I was still unable to complete as many repetitions as I would ordinarily expect.

I suspect my reduced carbohydrate intake may have contributed to the difference in performance, particularly if my muscle glycogen stores were lower than usual.

However, the residual fatigue from the preceding days and my recent illness are also plausible explanations.

An Unexpected Result with Dips

I also decided to change the order of my exercises.

Rather than proceeding directly to dips after the overhead press, I completed overhead triceps extensions using a cable machine.

I worked my triceps to failure before moving on to dips, anticipating that the additional fatigue would reduce my performance.

Interestingly, this wasn't the case.

I was still able to complete my usual repetitions and sets, despite having already exhausted my triceps during the preceding isolation exercise.

This was somewhat surprising, although I recognise that the two movements involve different mechanics and recruit other muscles to varying degrees.

It's possible that the fatigue accumulated during the cable extensions didn't limit the specific demands of the dips to the extent I had expected.

I eventually decided to finish the session rather than continue accumulating volume, as I was beginning to feel tired and had already accomplished what I wanted from the workout.

Nutrition and Future Sessions

Before training, I'd eaten some toast with honey, hoping that the additional carbohydrates would support my performance.

However, I began wondering whether a more substantial carbohydrate-rich meal the preceding evening might be beneficial, particularly when preparing for heavier sessions.

Carbohydrate digestion and glycogen replenishment are more complex than simply directing everything through the liver before it reaches the muscles, but the timing and overall availability of carbohydrates are nevertheless worth considering.

I'm interested in experimenting with this, especially while my overall training volume remains relatively high.

Reflections

What pleased me most about Tuesday was the fact that I'd retained so much of my strength despite the recent interruption to my training.

After nearly two weeks of illness, reduced appetite and considerably less exercise, I had expected a more noticeable decline.

Instead, I was able to reach my usual maximum on the flat dumbbell bench press and maintain my regular performance during dips.

There were some reductions elsewhere, particularly during the overhead press, but I don't think there's sufficient evidence to attribute these to any single factor.

I'm currently adjusting several variables simultaneously, including training frequency, volume, dietary intake and body weight.

That makes it difficult to isolate cause and effect, but it also provides an interesting opportunity to observe how my body responds to these changes over time.

For now, I'm satisfied with the progress and particularly pleased to have returned to heavier training without having lost much of the strength I'd previously developed.
